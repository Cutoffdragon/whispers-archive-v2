Comfortable in bed, behind the walls of my secure home - the doorbell rang. It was 11 AM. Groggy with residual dreams, I walked downstairs in a nightgown and slippers. I shielded my eyes from the glaring sun as I opened the door. Before me stood an unfamiliar man. He looked like a human, and seemed somewhat human. “Good morning, ma’am.” He swallowed. The word “ma’am” was fragmented - his voice trembled. Dark rings beleaguered his eyes. The pupils were shattered ornaments. Fidgeting, the strange man extended his hand. Then he immediately lowered it. His palm glistened with sweat in the noon sun. His hand vibrated like a twitching insect. 


“Can I help you?” I asked, my hand clinging to the doorknob like a weapon. His hands shifted. His lips mouthed empty words. Wrinkled jeans and a dirty flannel hung loose about his frail body. I prepared to close the door, sensing an unfamiliar danger. He put his hand against it, halting time for one dreadful moment.


“Ma’am -” He started with the same uneasy pronunciation, his voice cracking. “Would you, I have, television service - who, who is your current-”


“No thank you, have a nice day.” I cut him off. My hand pushed my front door. But his hand remained firm. The man rushed past me, into my house. It happened in the blink of an eye. He ascended my steps to the second floor, sprinting. I was initially puzzled, incredulous- then the fear settled like shock. I fled the house, seeking shelter with my closest neighbor. Calm and understanding, my neighbor called the police. Her husband obtained his pistol. Wielding the gun like a sergeant, he peered through the window of what used to be my home. He entered the living room, examining each turn and corner. All the while I stood in shock - my neighbor on the phone, her husband searching the house. 


“There’s a man… think he’s stealing… went upstairs… posed as a salesman…” The neighbor's words were fragmented, meaningless. Her husband exited the house. He was shaking his head and shrugging his shoulders. No intruder was found. They claimed that he likely escaped through a window, and we walked the house’s perimeter. All the windows were shut, just as I had left them. 


The police arrived, a squadron of six armed men, filing through the front door. No man or thing was found. Every inch, every corner had been examined. The closets, the basement, the attic. Even the cabinets and boxes were swept. But no intruder was revealed. They came to the same conclusion - the man escaped shortly after I fled the house. I was forced to accept this like antidepressants. Prescribed to it like a doubtful religion. Understandably, I was worried that the man would return - if he was not still hiding inside. For the next week, I stayed with some friends as expensive security equipment was installed in the home. There was no possibility that he was still within. Not unless he had hidden from the men who installed the cameras. I returned to a hostile house. The white paneling and yellow wallpaper were hostile and alien. Every piece of furniture was tainted by a stranger’s residue. No longer did I feel like a lonely, single twenty-six year old. The house felt like a fake replica built from putty. I walked through the hallways, sat in the kitchen, and stood in the bathroom. Something was not right.


As I ate dinner, I glanced at a cheap still life. Once it hung beside an antique clock. But it glared from a different wall altogether. There was little reason to believe that the security company had moved my painting. There was even less to believe that I had done it myself. I checked the new security footage through an app on my phone. It appeared the painting had been in that new spot since the recordings began. That night I struggled to sleep, unable to understand why the man would have moved that painting. In my nightmares, I saw him in the house. He stood next to me as I cleaned the dishes and vacuumed the rug. He just stared with those dark, sunken eyes. His hands shook as if an electrical current ran through them. Frozen like a victim of war, ruminating in flashbacks of murder and entropy. I awoke to a loud bang. Between sleep and reality, there is a moment - a thin line where reality blends with the uncanny. And in that split second, I heard a door close within that house.


For the next week, I noticed subtle differences in various arrangements. Food lay half-eaten in the fridge. Food that I did not touch. Doors were left ajar. The toilet would be unflushed. One night as I rummaged through the security footage, I saw someone in the recording. It appeared to be myself, eating a piece of leftover London broil at two in the morning. It was walking like a zombie, as if sleepwalking - a character in my clothes, with hair that resembled my own. It shambled into the kitchen, eating the cold steak like a beggar eats trash.


Though I remained wary of the guest, I never encountered him. And he never committed anything directly against me. By the time I turned twenty seven, his presence was chronic. I thought about him all day, every day. He was the first thing I thought of in the morning, and the last thing I thought of at night. I found his bread crumbs throughout the house. Each time I would check the cameras, I would see that strange figure. Wearing my clothes, my hair, possessing a face shrouded by darkness. Always in the early hours of the morning, when I should have been asleep. Advice was of no use - my friends told me I was overthinking the situation. My therapist explained that my mind was manifesting delusions. No one believed that someone was impersonating and stalking me, just inches away at all times.


Nothing palpable ever came from my paranoia. Life passed in years consisting of strife and suspicion. One year passed just as quickly and gruesomely as five. At night, I would wake up to see a dark figure standing in the corner of my room. My therapist wrote it off as sleep paralysis - a condition where one wakes up functionally paralyzed, tormented by a shadow visage. But I could move very well on those nights. And I could see the trembling hands of the shadow, just like they shook on the very day he showed up at my door.


Ten years passed, and I was getting old, turning thirty-seven. Already my hair had greyed from stress. My face appeared to be fifty years old, with severe wrinkles, crows feet, and intense bags under my eyes. As I looked in the mirror, I thought I resembled him. Eventually, I acclimated to his presence. We talked sometimes. I would eat dinner alone. Cold, and alone. And I would hear a faint call from the living room. 


“How is it?” the voice asked - that same, quivering voice that questioned me about television.


“It’s cold.” I would reply apathetically.


On nights of extreme mental duress, I would yell at him. “Kill me!” I would beg. “Kill me now! Do it already! Kill me however you want!” He would laugh silently and mockingly in response.


I remember I was taking a lukewarm shower. The water lacked the heat to nullify my pain and loneliness. Behind the shower curtains, a shadow walked into the bathroom. The silhouette was clad in my clothes, wearing my hair. Fallen hairs that he had picked up and pieced together into a deranged, lice-infested wig. He was whispering something to me. Whispering incessantly as I cowered on the shower floor, begging him to leave me alone, to leave the house forever.


“I am you. You are nothing without me. Without you, I am nothing.” The chants droned on  in cryptic echoes.


Moving from the house was the only solution. I was already forty five. Though that home was an heirloom inherited from my mother, I sought to rid myself of it. But my financial situation was limited by my inability to hold a stable job. My therapist discouraged moving, claiming the effigy could follow me wherever I went. She still believed that it was all a fantasy. An indication of deeper trauma. I cursed at my therapist, and would never see her again.


I would like to say I was all alone - but it was me and the man. I walked down the hallway late one night, my brain separated from reality. The attic was open - I looked up into the dark portal. He stood within, invisible save for his black silhouette blotted against the darkness. He watched me, and I watched back. After a tense, frozen moment - I crumbled. Screaming, I sprinted down the hallway, running for the phone. I called the police - I told them the man was in my house, I knew he was there, please help me, please get him out.


And they just replied with their same sarcastic response. “This again?”, “Ma’am, we just checked last night.”, “You need help.”


Children ridiculed my name and the very idea of me. Rumors spread throughout town. I became the crazy grey haired lady with a strange man in her house. News articles were written across the state. Online videos were published detailing my life story. Internet archives recorded my name and my history with the strange man. Sightseers and tourists would visit the house, flashing pictures just beyond the property line. That house, which had once been mine, became a landmark. I entered the annals of psychotic mentalities. I couldn’t call the police anymore. They threatened to prosecute me, to commit me to an intense mental institution.


“Don’t worry. You’re not alone. I am you. You are me.” I heard from a dark room as I sobbed on the ground.


On a sleepless night, I heard the television droning from the living room. I wandered through the hallway in a trance. My hair was a wiry grey mess. My night gown was tattered and unwashed. The faint colors of television leered down the hallway like a ghastly, faded rendition of aurora borealis. In front of the television, an outline sat in my favorite seat, his arms comfortably at rest. Pensively, I sat down on the couch across from him. There he was, wearing my clothes, wearing the wig made of my hair. His hands trembling, his lips mumbling to themselves. He turned his head to me.


“Do you like this show?” He asked, gesturing to the television set with a remote. Slowly, I turned to the television. A news broadcast featuring two depressed anchors played. They spoke of incoming tornados, earthquakes ravaging the west, and impending nuclear war. “Yes.” I replied. “It’s ok.”


The man nodded with contentment. “You can sit with me. We’re never going to be apart.”


My remembrance of the program was foggy. The news anchors were sullen and tearing. Me and the man talked like friends, like he was a relative. Closer than my mother, closer than my father. As I came to accept him - as I stopped calling the police, stopped checking around every corner, my stress abated. But this radical change of mind came too late to prevent fatal damage. I began to cough up blood. One day, I passed out in the shower. I experienced raging coughing fits followed by bouts of unconsciousness. It was terminal cancer, incurable, a death sentence. Induced by the harrowing stress plaguing me for the previous twenty years. The cancer was untreatable - no amount of radiation therapy or surgery was going to save me. The tumor was massive, suffocating my organs, nullifying my bodily functions.


As I lay on my deathbed, I asked the nurse to leave me alone and turn the lights off. She accepted reluctantly, abandoning me in the dark hospital room. I sighed in deference to death. A creaking door opened from the corner of the room. From that closet, the dark shadow of the man emerged. He walked to the side of my bed, dragging a chair along with him. As he sat down, he took my hand in his. I could feel the claminess, the trembling as I writhed in great pain, moments away from dying. He rubbed my hand softly, gently.


“I suppose you want me to apologize, to say that I caused all of this.” He began sadly. “And perhaps I did. But what would you have been without me? Who would you have met, who would you have become? And inversely… who would I ever have been without you? I was a mess when I first encountered you. I still am. But you were so… normal. Our two lives, trajectories toward an indiscernible, unsatisfying fate, have breached each other's gravity with the force of a meteor. We were at war for most of our lives. Carrying each other like luggage. But is that not how it was always supposed to be? Is it that which is good, which is normal that defines us? That makes life remarkable? Or is it that which stalks us from the dark?”


The man released my hand. As my surroundings dissolved into white light, his shadow was the last fading image of reality. Beside me, the life support machine buzzed with a screeching alarm as the heart rate monitor flatlined.
