Football, girlfriends, expensive clothing. I never fit the prerequisites for popularity. My lunch table bore one resident - myself. My classmates made fun of my interests: model airplanes, obscure operating systems, and most of all, classical music. In a modernity composed of slurred lyrics repeating meaningless lines, classical was an echo to times of purposeful computation. Pop hits and raps made with no other intention than to fuel a high, to stoke the flame of an anarchic party were anathema to what made the art of sound beautiful. I may have been alone my entire life if I hadn’t met Allen and Jeremy in band class. To me, they may have been the only people in the world with an open mind. And while not originally fans of classical, they took interest in my world. With little persuasion, they were converted to higher tastes in music, composed of fine instruments and lyrical deprivation.


We formed a club called ‘The Undead Composers’, as we believed we were literally resurrecting classical music. Allan and Jeremy would come to my house on the weekends. Rather than play sports or video games, we would lock ourselves in my dark basement and listen to the cornerstones of classical. We did not talk during our sessions. We did not bob our heads or dance. We sat in darkness, the small sunshine of an afternoon day peering through the slim basement window, as we listened to the music with closed eyes. We formed a triangle of humans in cheap chairs, a serenity of silence. It was Jeremy who proposed the idea of ‘Classical Canvas’. We would listen to a song in silence, and afterward we would draw any images provoked by the symphony. Classical was the only genre of music that could bring entire landscapes to mind, eidetic sequences of the most beautiful fantasies. After only the first few games of Classical Canvas, we made a shocking discovery: our interpretations were all relatively similar. I would draw a picture of a steadfast horse galloping over a sun bleached meadow. Jeremy would draw three horses grazing from a similar meadow. Allan would draw a horse at rest, the sun setting upon the green fields. 


We were shocked by the idea that sounds could create coordination in our minds, as if our sessions were conducive to telepathy. But Allan proposed a theory - one that made sense. We were simply drawing images that we had already seen before. Dubious of the idea, I eventually came to realize that he was correct. After browsing an old picture book from my childhood, I found the very image of the horse I had recreated. It was an amazing concept: certain combinations of sound and instrument caused our minds to recall the same kind of image. This breakthrough inspired us to start our own podcast in college, which we named after our club: ‘The Undead Composers’.


As we grew older, we gained proficiency in a number of instruments and technologies. Me, Jeremy, and Allan were each creating our own classical songs. A modern revival of classical, which we would showcase on our podcast. Before long we developed a cult following. We would invite guest speakers to interviews, and have them listen to our compositions for the first time. In doing so, we recreated our long-time game of ‘Classical Canvas’. Our guest would be invited into a dark room, where they would close their eyes and listen to the song. Then, they would be asked to draw any image or world that came to mind. We found that our songs tended to reflect our individual personalities, and provoked images that were in line with such. I was the quiet, reflective individual. My songs were harmonious, often invoking ideas of serene natural landscapes. Jeremy, always upbeat and eccentric, inspired themes of revelry and glory with his creations. Allan was an oddball - brooding and morose, with an inclination towards the occult. His songs were known to be dark, depressing. In some cases, this stirred strong emotions in the listener. In other cases… they would express profound fear. Paranoia.


We graduated college - all three of us musical majors. ‘Undead Composers’ had risen to the peak of its popularity, and we were all making enough money to relax for the rest of our lives. Unfortunately, the quality of our music plateaued. No longer were we increasing in skill and quality. Our songs lacked originality. Jeremy fell hard on drugs and alcohol. Allan suffered from a deep, disturbing brain fog. As if something horrible was haunting the man. The glory days of Undead Composers was locked in a tomb of the past. I could barely get my best friends out of the house anymore. Me and Jeremy’s family held an intervention, sending him to rehabilitation for his drug addiction. To my disappointment, Allan was absent for the event. After Jeremy recovered, I expressed my deep concerns to him. He only suggested what I already knew: that Allan was deeply depressed, and perhaps even schizophrenic. Nonetheless, I proposed that we visit our friend, and provide him with any comfort needed. I began to fear that before long, Allan would eventually take his own life.


Allan was abnormally pale. His hair was a dark, greasy mess, and he looked as if he had not slept in months. It took little coercion - Allan broke down before us, begging for help. Not from himself, but from something else. Something sinister. Allan brought us into his dark attic, and we sat in a triangle. With our eyes closed, he played what he claimed was ‘his masterpiece’. The symphony started out with a high pitched violin, raucous, discordant. It would break into intervals of silence, leaving the paranoid of when the exact breaks in noise would be. But at a certain point - I can not recall - the most disturbing sequence one can imagine comes to mind. A frizzy haired man in a long black coat sitting at a piano. A loner in an old, antique house, the wallpaper dirty and peeling with neglect. He played notes in harmony with the song, leaving one to feel like they were in the very jaws of hell, as if a shadowed man stood directly behind them. Then, slowly, as the song reaches its demented crescendo, the man turns to look at me. His face is deformed, perfectly white. His eye sockets are hollow, perfect black holes. His teeth are sharp and yellow, decorations of a twisted smile, the corners of his lips stretching too far. 


Both me and Jeremy shrieked, standing up, begging Allan to turn the lights back on. We spared no time in telling him of our interpretation. With strenuous effort, all three of us drew what we had seen - Allan seemingly having the worst time of all of us. When we compared images, I nearly fainted. All three of us had drawn the exact same man, in the exact same house. Turning towards us with those festering eye sockets, the smile that devours children. But the most sinister idea of all had yet to be breached. The fact that at some point in our lives, all three of us had seen that exact image before. Just as I had seen the depiction of the idyllic horse, sprinting across the sun tinged meadow.


We called upon some old guests, longtime friends from the history of Undead Composers. Sitting them in Allan’s dark attic, we turned off the lights and played Allan’s song. Out of the eight people we invited, we turned on the lights to find three of them unconscious, passed out from fear. Two had sprinted from the attic, running wild and shoeless into the street, never to be heard from again. Two had curled into fetal positions, wetting themselves. And one shrieked violently, cursing at us, lunging at us with a stray piece of wire as we intervened in the attic. Of the four people we were able to retain, all four of their drawn interpretations matched. All four depicted the deranged pianist, the true depiction of an Undead Composer.


I had become like Allan. A depressed recluse, hiding home all day, wondering when and how myself, and all those other people had seen an image of that pianist. Allan was unresponsive. He did not answer my phone calls, and any attempt to reach out was met with cold ignorance. Jeremy called me one day, frantic, panicking. Demanding that I meet him at a diner. When I arrived, he was shivering, sweating, smoking a cigarette with a hand that trembled violently. He spoke in parables, his words moving too fast for comprehension. Talking about how the pianist was watching, watching him from that old house on a deserted prairie, stalking him in his dreams. I affirmed his belief - as I had never lost the image either. Every day I saw that demented face, the razor sharp teeth. Jeremy would not calm down - he grabbed my throat from across the table. His eyes were bloodshot, wild with disbelief. After I finally broke free of his grasp, everyone in the diner staring at us, Jeremy ran from the building. He sprinted down the sidewalk, screaming, laughing and crying simultaneously. I would never see him alive again. Jeremy’s mother called me one night, crying softly. Her son had fallen back on the drugs. She had found him lifeless, dead from an overdose.


Allan did not show up to the funeral. I was calling him multiple times per day, sending him emails, contacting his relatives. Every attempt was met with a dead end. Before long, I could not take the anticipation. I drove to Allan’s house on a restless night, speeding recklessly, uncaring of whether I lived or died. His house was lightless, showing no signs of habitation. The grass was brown, his plants shriveled husks. I walked around the perimeter, looking for any sign that someone, something lived within those walls. But the house was as silent as a crypt. I only thought of Jeremy. That Allan had met the same fate - that he had succumbed to his own desire for death. I broke in through the window. Scouring the house, calling his name, it was apparent: Allan was gone. The house had been deserted for quite some time. The sparse articles of food in the fridge were rotten, expired. The bedroom was a mess, loose articles of clothing flung throughout. There was only one area I had not yet searched. The attic, the last spot we had played Classical Canvas.


Images of the deathly pianist haunted me as I climbed into the pitch dark attic. I fumbled for the light switch, reaching for it as if my life depended on it. Because I could not stand the darkness of that attic, because within, I thought I may see him - that demented man, turning slowly to me, his mouth grinning, dripping with the blood of innocents. But to my relief, I saw nothing. Only the empty attic, cold and forgotten. One memento was out of place. Atop one of the cheap, foldable chairs, still arranged in a triangle, was a small cassette tape. Atop it was a piece of scotch tape. Written on the tape in red marker, was ‘To The Undead Composers’.


Next to the triangle of chairs was the old cassette deck we used to play our songs on. Ripping off the piece of scotch tape, I placed Allan’s Tape in the cassette deck. Just as I was about to hit play, I paused. Because one circumstance was still out of place. The light was still on. Trembling, fearing for my very life, I turned off the lights and pressed play. I closed my eyes as I withstood Allan’s Symphony, the darkest orchestration I had ever heard. And from that discordant collection of unreal sounds, the following image was painted on the canvas of my mind:


The dark pianist, his hair drooping to the dusty wooden floor of his old antique home, dripping with blood. His face, stooping to the ground, made guttural groans over a body. A familiar body - my long lost friend, Allan. With his razor sharp teeth, he ripped flesh from Allan’s body, feasting on his cheeks, his stomach, his tongue-


And then the face shot upward - looking straight at me - before he lunged for me.
