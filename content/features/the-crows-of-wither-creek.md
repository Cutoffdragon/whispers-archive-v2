I used to think it was Uncle N wandering the corn fields at night. His ghastly lantern, undulating like a deathly ferryman. But after we found his body, that lantern continued to sway. I always watched it from my bedroom window. In my nightmares of the occult, I saw it as well. A mystical air has pervaded my family’s farm since I was a child. And when I found that note in Gerta’s Haunt, a plot to murder my family, I came to realize why. It was a dark time for us. Never had I seen Father so sullen, so deep in his cups. Since Mother died, Uncle N was all he had left. They had operated the farm together since they were boys. The farm itself was an heirloom passed through our bloodline. I remember the stories Uncle N used to tell me. Tales of capricious deities lurking in the grass, demanding a feast of children in exchange for a bountiful harvest. Father would scold him for polluting my mind with such nonsense - but those were some of the more grounded tales. Uncle N’s darkest fascination was the crows.


They circled the sky at dawn, blotting the sun like locusts. There were hundreds, perhaps thousands on darker mornings. Uncle N would run outside, cursing them atop a bare patch of dirt. My Uncle was eccentric, to put it lightly. Father had a family; he had mother, and he had me. Uncle N. never had a wife or offspring. He never had anyone but us. Rarely did he leave the farm. In spite of the murderous crows, Uncle N developed a strange hobby. A craft only he held an ornate eye for. Building scarecrows. The hobby was ritualistic - Constructing an effigy from straw, tattered clothing, and vegetation. He would seclude himself for days in Gerta’s Haunt, working tirelessly, forgoing health and hygiene. In his completed works, I saw familiar faces; dead ringers. Days after a death in the family - Uncle N. takes to the Haunt. When he emerges, a new scarecrow guards the fields. My little brother Joseph died in a tractor accident at just six years old. Soon after, a scarecrow bore his face, his smile. At sunset, I always watched Mother's scarecrow. It stood closer to our house, to our heart, than any other who had passed.


On the night we found Uncle N’s body, I saw his lantern swaying through the fields. I wanted to ask Father about it the next morning, but he had locked himself in Gerta’s Haunt. Once a stable, that old, creaky barn lingered in the distance like a fading wickerman. It was a crumbling, mouse infested abomination. Uncle N always said the Haunt was cursed. That the ghosts of our ancestors convened within during the witching hour. There, they deliberated upon the fate of our family. Who would live, and who would die. Which crops would flourish, and which would decay. Father always hated the Haunt. He and Uncle N debated whether to tear it down or preserve it. And though Father succumbed, he still kept a distance of fifty feet from The Haunt at all times. But on that morning after we found Uncle N dead, Father was locked in The Haunt. And I knew why.


Dinner was usually a session of occult sermons from Uncle N. He spoke constantly of impending death and spirits. “When I die…” He would say, looking at my father with burning eyes. “You know what to do.”


John Bo Callahan was the only other witness to those conversations. He was like a brother to me, though we were not blood related. Plucked from the binds of poverty as a child, he was our toughest farmhand.  Only two years older than me, we grew up as play mates. He was my confidant, the only one I could express my worries to. We used to take long walks in the corn fields, passing the scarecrows as we talked. I asked him all the questions posed by our farm’s mysteries.


“Why is Uncle N. so strange?” I would wonder innocently.


John Bo walked confidently with a piece of hay between his lips. Bearing a grimace, he spat on the ground. “You know why Uncle N never left the farm? Why he has no children?” John Bo packed a fresh clump of tobacco in his lower lip. “He’s a necrolyte, a child of witches and warlocks dancing in the woodland. I’ve followed him in the night, I’ve seen the bonfires, the rituals.” John Bo spat. “There’s two kinds of people in this world. The tax paying folk, eating and sleeping, sustaining our bodies. And those who dance with hooved creatures. Miscreants. Me, you, your old man: we’re farmers. Uncle N is something else, a man without a place.” Again, he spat. We walked past a hooded scarecrow wearing an executioner’s mask. Like haunted portraits, their gaze seemed to follow anyone as they walked by. Engraved in the scarecrow’s wooden post were carved runes.  


“What do those symbols say?” I nudged John Bo.


“Their name. That one’s your great grandpa. At least, that’s what Uncle N told me. He could read those symbols like they were his primary language.”


Caws echoed, and wings flapped above us. A black tornado of crows spied on us with hateful red eyes. They were large and unnatural - the size of vultures and condors. And though they sought to pluck us from the ground like rodents, they never came near the fields. They were terrified of the scarecrows. Still John Bo looked up, the piece of hay, falling softly from his lips.


“C’mon, let’s head back.” John Bo said with concern. He put his arm around my back.


We walked quickly, looking at our feet as we walked. “John Bo, why was Uncle N. afraid of the crows?”


John Bo glanced to the side. “Look at them. Vultures. If it weren’t for these scarecrows, I think they’d run this place dryer than a desert. Turn us to dust, pick the marrow from our bones. Uncle N. said crows are a vile race. They pilfer our crops because they want us to starve, because they detest humanity and seek our extinction.” John Bo spat, emptying the clump of tobacco from his lips.


On our way back, we saw the oldest scarecrow - the wooden post splintering, the old flannel disintegrating. John Bo admired it distantly, the pumpkin head rife with holes and rot. A soft smile graced his lips. “Old Chester. The founder of our farm... The progenitor of your folks-” John Bo suddenly glanced downward. Perhaps there was a sharp pain, a sadness that he had no real relatives of his own. But we were his family - blood was merely a liquid to be spilled.


“Your Uncle N,  he…” John Bo stared pensively at the crows.


“He what?” I pressed eagerly. “What happened to him? Father never told me, he didn’t want to talk about it. I only know he found him in Gerta’s Haunt…”


John Bo kneeled down. His expression, at first serious, was slowly consumed by depression. “He… he was murdered.” He muttered quietly. I gasped. Tears flooded my eyes, and I wanted to accuse John Bo of lying. “At least… had to be. Your dad, he found him… sliced open. Gutted like a pig.” John Bo stood up, taking my cheeks in his hand, holding them tightly. “Don’t tell him I told you that. He didn’t want you to know that, you hear me?”


I was in a state of shock, and the tears had broken free like the bursting of a dam. I released myself from John Bo’s grasp and ran toward the farmhouse sobbing. Along the way, I crossed paths with the contemptuous shadow of Gerta’s Haunt. I sprinted past my mother’s scarecrow and the runes which spelled her forgotten name. Blazing up the steps of the front porch, I burst through the front door and up the stairs. I locked myself in my room until the evening, pressing my face into my pillow.


Not long into a destitute nap, I awoke to sobs from the front yard. Evening twilight pierced my window with orange flares, like hell was rising from space. I ran to my window. Father was kneeling in the dead patch of dirt in front of our porch. I had not seen him in nearly a day.


“Curse you…” He muttered. A trembling, twisted look demented his face. His lips quivered, and his eyes twitched. His face paled beyond white, nearly blue. Next to Mother's scarecrow stood a new, unfamiliar effigy. The pumpkin head contained a jagged mouth with sharp, monstrous teeth. It was not a healthy or ripe pumpkin. It was misshapen, like squash harvested from unfathomable depths. The eyes slanted sharply, locked in an eternal guise of hatred. Black, ripped robes clung to the body, flapping in the ominous wind. Atop the head was a pointed black hood. The very hood I had seen rising above the corn stalks at night. 


“Curse you N!” Father cried. “Look what you’ve made me do! Look at what you’ve done to me!” Father broke into tears, his face falling to the dirt. John Bo burst from the front door of the farmhouse, kneeling down to console him. The crows circled above, cawing and laughing at our decaying family. Oh, how much Father detested those scarecrows. And yet, I knew he had been burdened with the task of creating one himself - fulfilling his brother’s dying wish. Just as I was afraid of cockroaches and John Bo was afraid of snakes, Scarecrows were the bane of Father’s existence. Toward the end of Uncle N’s life, it was a source of tension between him and Father. Liquor took a greater hold of Father, causing him to grow angry and irrational. He would march through the cornfields, cursing and pushing the scarecrows with a shotgun slung over his shoulder. One day, drunk as a baboon, he lifted his weapon and pulled the trigger, obliterating the scarecrow of my cousin Aaron. I was spared the scene - but I heard the blast. I heard the arguing and the fighting that followed. Uncle N charged my father, tackling him to the ground. They wrestled like a pit of dogs, scratching and bashing, screaming and threatening each other. Afterward, I saw them hose blood off themselves. An unusual amount of blood.


John Bo led Father inside and rested him on the couch, placing a damp cloth on his forehead. Before long, Father fell asleep, whispering to himself madly. With John Bo preoccupied, I slipped into the darkness outdoors. Curiously, I approached Uncle N’s scarecrow, growing deeply disturbed. There was no symmetry between Uncle’s craftsmanship and Father’s butchery. I only wished that Uncle N could have created his own scarecrow. That his spirit could return to save our farm. 


A gale of wind slammed the large door of Gerta’s Haunt against its frame. Never had the old barn been left unlocked - but in his hysteria, Father had forgotten to close it. Within me sparked irresistible curiosity. On bare feet, I approached the old barn. As I grew closer, dried stalks of corn rustled and whispered. Before I entered the Haunt, the front door of the farmhouse was thrust open. I jerked my head around, seeing the shadowed silhouette of John Bo on the porch. He was staring directly at me. I had been caught in an act I was not entirely cognizant of committing. John Bo would never let me in the Haunt. He would rather tie me up in the cellar than let me witness the dark secrets within. But if his ignorance could grant me passage, so it did. Rather than chase me or yell at me, he sullenly turned around, receding into the farmhouse. The occurrence was unreal, as if a delusion had preoccupied a split second of reality. I wandered into the darkness of Gerta’s Haunt, and closed the large doors.


Blue moonlight illuminated the decrepit interior. Only once had I been inside the Haunt. When I was young, I wandered inside during a game of hide and seek with John Bo. All that can be recalled was a strange presence within; something solid and obscured laying on a wooden table. I walked past an empty pen filled with browned bales of hay. The old pen of Gerta, the prize pig who had died a mysterious death. It was her name that branded the Haunt. I remember her soft stare and her innocent eyes. Eyes that foresaw inevitable death. Whether hers or ours, I would never know. I came across my hiding spot from that fateful day. Within a stall, where once horses stood, was only a pile of loose hay. It was hollow within, forming a dark cave of isolation. Within that cave, I hid from John Bo as a child, my little heart beating playfully. Parting the hay, preparing to crawl inside, I took in the darkness of The Haunt. Atop every box and on every windowsill were half-melted candles. If lit by fire, they could burn the barn from their heat alone. Giggling with childlike nostalgia, I entered the hollow bale of hay. Holding my knees to my chest, I exhaled a sigh of relief. I closed my eyes, and let my mind run like a soothing bath. My toes wiggled, feeling the dirt, the hay, and - a piece of paper beneath. I knew from the feel of it, from the crisp way it crunched under pressure, that a strange sheet of paper lay within the cave.


Poking my head outside the sanctuary, I looked for any sign of Father, John Bo, and even Uncle N. But it seemed that only darkness and dark things inhabited The Haunt. I crawled from the hay with the piece of paper in my hand. By the blue light of the moon, I unfolded what appeared to be a letter or a log. The writing within was indecipherable, but the letters posed a strange familiarity to my mind. It was written in the same runes and symbols that lined the wooden post of each scarecrow. A soft curse escaped my mouth, and I devised a plan to translate the note the very next day.


Crows darkened the next morning’s sky. On the front porch, John Bo was sipping wooden liquid from a muddy glass. It was the same beverage father would drown himself in. Flocks of crows ebbed and flowed like waves of a blackened ocean. John Bo observed them like a philosopher, slowly sipping from his glass. Next to mother’s scarecrow, where the visage of Uncle N stood the day before, was emptiness.


“What happened to the scarecrow?” I asked John Bo. He seemed unwilling to register my voice… unable to take notice of anything but the crows, the crows. I snapped my fingers. John Bo spat and shook his head. “Your father took it behind The Haunt. Said he was going to put it down like a rabid dog.”


I searched the grounds for my father. He was nowhere amidst the wilting farm or the Haunt. My hands trembled with anger. My fists balled themselves with vindication. I only thought of what I would do to him if he ever harmed Mother’s scarecrow.


The cawing of crows was deafening, drowning out the sounds of wind and insects. John Bo leaned backward. “There’s been more of them since he… left us.” He whispered, taking another sip of the brown poison. I wanted to tell John Bo about the piece of cryptic paper and the candles in the Haunt. But as I turned back to him, it appeared he had drifted into a drunken haze on the steps of the porch. Inside the farmhouse, I obtained a piece of paper and a dull pencil. I visited each scarecrow, each relative, writing down their names and their corresponding runes. I started with my mother, then visited my cousins, my grandparents, my in-laws. There were fewer scarecrows than I remembered. Though certainly, there were no fewer dead relatives. When the crops were green and bountiful, those harrowing statues littered every corner of the farm. In a dead patch of dirt, where once stood the scarecrow of my Aunt Nicole, a large crow pecked at loose corn. I shrieked, leaping backward into foliage. Never had I seen one on the ground, so close to the farm house. The crow looked up at me with its hooked beak, sharp enough to rend flesh. The red eyes, devoid of pupils, stared at me with gluttonous hunger. It cawed - a malefic, distorted caw. I ran deep through the fields without looking back. Just before I breached the crow’s wilderness - no man’s land -  I nearly ran into the tallest, furthest scarecrow. Chester, our founder. Its red, faded flannel flapped with the wind. Quickly looking over my shoulder, I could not see the monstrous crow I had fled from. I recorded Chester’s name and runes on my paper.


“What are you doing out here?” A deep voice emerged over my shoulder. I jerked around thinking that maybe - I would see Uncle N. His white eyes, his face wrought with lines of worry. But it was Father’s voice that bore the resemblance of his fallen brother.


“I, I…” My mind was bare of explanation. Father’s hands were dark brown. His shirt was stained red. I crept slowly backwards, confused and frightened.


“Don’t stay out past dark.” He said. But he was not even looking at me when he spoke. Father stared straight at Chester’s scarecrow. His face twisted from distant fogginess to animosity. “And don’t go near that barn…” He said with a voice that seemed miles away. Father turned and walked back to the farmhouse. His steps were militaristic. His arms did not sway.


Panicked breaths escaped me. I took a few moments to calm myself, clinging to Chester like a sentinel. The sun set slowly over the horizon. With my back to Chester’s post, I slowly slid to the ground, and began creating a cipher. From my recordings, I was able to translate nineteen runes into alphabetic characters. I attempted to translate the log onto a new sheet of paper. Letter after letter, I filled in the gaps of my cipher. Each sentence further cursed the mystery. When I had finished, a great sense of fear overcame me. The sun had set, darkness reigned, and I had a complete translation of the mysterious log. All around me, the stalks of corn began rustling, whispering. Sprinting into the safety of our farm house, I read my translation under a flickering lamplight:


March 25th, The Council Convenes


They amass, the blight. They wane the minds of the Creator and his kin. The Council convenes once more to name a new threat - The Destroyer. He who has succumbed to their influence, who slaughters more of our kind. Another one has fallen by his hands yet. Through his hands they obliterate us. They wage unholy war upon the dying Creek. 




March 27th, The Council Convenes


Another of our kind has fallen to the Destroyer. From dust and carnage, The Destroyer once brought life to the unsown fields. From flesh and blood, The Creator brings the guardians to their posts. But the Destroyer has fallen. He undoes the Creator’s work, and he threatens the sanctity of Wither Creek.




Many of the log entries were from the previous few nights. Each was written like a melancholic poem. Strange terms were repeatedly mentioned - the death of Wither Creek, The Creator, and The Destroyer. It was the latest and final entry that left me shivering like a frozen ghost.


March 29th The Council Convenes,


May the Creator Rest in peace. Through a deathly gambit, the Destroyer has won. And should the creek be left to wither and rot, The family is to die. One by one we shall pluck them in the night with our silver scythes. The destroyer, and any who may fall under the influence of the blight above, must perish. The young boy. The young girl. As the night is our domain, it is then we shall harvest their flesh and their beating hearts. And from their likeliness we shall fashion a new creator, one of our own kind.


It was the thirty-first of March. Should the Council adhere to their pattern, they would convene on that very night. My fingers crushed the paper. What had just been read - witnessed - was a plot to murder my family. The Creator, The Destroyer. Given what I had read, those titles could only point to two people. People I knew very well. Yet who was it that composed the Council? I only thought of John Bo’s tales on Uncle N. How he was born deep in the woods, how he retained the pagan sorcery of that brooding place. There was only one way I could discover the Council’s identity. As they gathered by candlelight, I would spy on them within the bale of hay - my cave. I would come to know exactly who met by purple candle light, and why they sought to rip our hearts from us.


All evening, John Bo and Father argued in the living room. I desperately sought to tell them of my discovery. But I now saw Father in a different light; one of hostility and instability. In the midst of their argument, I walked across the twilight fields of Wither Creek, entering Gerta’s Haunt. As I slid into my hiding spot, the sun sank beneath grey fields of corn. My breaths were heavy in the cool night, sending mist forth as if my soul were dissipating. I was shaking, clutching my knees. Within the cave was perfect darkness. Hours passed, and no one came looking for me. Yells protruded from the farm house until my last two relatives exhausted themselves. The lights went out, and pitch darkness engorged the land like a dome of oblivion. Peering my head through the hay, I saw undulating fires. Like massive glow bugs approaching the barn, many lights swayed within the cornfields. Obscure figures in black cloaks entered The Haunt. They looked strikingly similar to Uncle N when he walked those very fields. Two strangers gathered within, joined by four more. As they stood in a perfect circle, I could make nothing of their faces. 


They spoke in hushed whispers. Their voices were inhuman, wrought with reverberations like effigies in crystal balls. Much of what they said was lost to me, absorbed by the suffocating atmosphere. From their most coherent whispers, I gathered harrowing information:


“Preparations are in order.” A sunken voice started.


“We can wait no longer.” Another chimed in with ghastly urgency.


“One by one we fall. The Destroyer and his brood must die by our hands.”


“If not, the war is forfeit. Wither Creek will belong to them.”


“When? We have waited long enough… tonight they die…”


“Patience… We will endure until tomorrow night… When the moon is full, we may harvest its power.”


“Yes…”


“We should have acted on Halloween… when the creator was still with us…”


“Long live the council. Long live Wither Creek.”


The council lifted their candles upward, revealing their faces. Beneath black hoods were orange heads with carved orifices. I saw them, my aunt, my cousin, Chester. All of them, scarecrows, the scarecrows created by Uncle N. As they filed out of the barn like a dark line of pilgrims, I searched for Mother’s scarecrow, desperate to see her face. But she was not with them. The council returned to the cornfields, falling back upon their posts, returning to inanimacy. In the wake of their revelations, I could not move. I could not think. There was only one night until they acted upon us. One night until the final full moon. These beings were the legacy of Uncle N. But had he truly meant to harm us? Him, and his long walks in the woods, conversing with covens from the unreachable depths of wilderness. This was the power borne of my uncle, that estranged man with no ties of his own. And on the night of the full moon, their power would undoubtedly be the strongest. There was no other option - I had to tell John Bo. I had to tell my father.


I spent a majority of the night fighting myself. Debating whether my family would take me seriously, debating whether I should just leave everything behind. When I was brave enough to emerge from The Haunt, sunlight was rising upon Wither Creek. I saw my father standing at his bedroom window, shirtless and bearded. His eyes were wide open, and he ground his teeth violently. The sky rumbled like a hurricane was coagulating. But it was the flapping of wings, the congregation of crows above. I refused to look upward as my father was. I thought that I could hear them speaking to me. A parasite crawling down my ear, eating its way to my brain.


“They come for us!” My father yelled, his torso hanging out the window. “Caw! Caw! Caw!” He imitated. It was apparent - that look in his eyes like shattered glass. The disheveled appearance, the lack of hygiene. His sanity was forfeit. “Caw! Caw! Caw!”


I burst through the front door. John Bo stood in the living room, gazing upstairs. A glass of brown liquid dangled loosely from his numb fingers. This was my protector, the man who had made me feel safe as a child. Never had I thought he could be so afraid. I clutched his shoulder, concerned for my father. “Caw! Caw!” Continued the madman upstairs.


“He’s lost it.” John Bo whispered, his gaze lost in the shadowed second floor. “What is happening? How I wish your uncle were here…”


Screaming, I dropped to my knees, shaking John Bo violently. In a burst of breath I confessed everything I had discovered - the piece of paper from the Haunt, the candles, the Council’s meeting, and the plot to murder our family. Urgency returned to his eyes. I could see his pupils contract, the veins in his face pulse.


“We have to leave.” He said. “Now.”


Before I could rise to my feet, John Bo ran up the stairs. I followed him upward. He ran to the doorway of Father’s bedroom. Rather than turn to face us, Father continuously leaned out the window, flapping his arms like wings.


“Listen to what you’ve done.” John Bo said to the wild, shirtless man. Beyond Father, crows darkened the sky like black flies. Father turned around with the look of a beast. The man and his adopted son glared at each other. They were tense, serious, as if they were about to draw weapons on each other.


“They’re coming for us. Probably at sun down.” John Bo began. “All because of you… you! We need to leave now, this instant. We could never stand against them, not during a full moon. Uncle N would never let us.”


Father’s eyes darted to me. His pupils resembled primal rubies from an ancient temple. “No…” he muttered. “This is my farm, always has been. I’ll never let it fall to those straw men.” Father stomped to the closet beside his bed. His lips ceaselessly mouthed ‘caw, caw’. Reaching in the closet, he obtained a double barrel shotgun. Father marched out of the room, brushing us aside with his shoulder. John Bo pursued him, urging him to help us, to come back to sanity. Locked in futile pleas, they emerged onto the dead patch of dirt in front of our house. Directly in front of them stood Mother’s scarecrow. All the while I followed, spectating like a prisoner in a straightjacket.


“I can’t let you do this.” John Bo begged, tears streaming down his cheeks. That man had never cried before. The tough farmhand, abandoned as a child, molded to the likeliness of hostile streets. “We need the scarecrows. You know what would happen without them…”


The murder of crows swirled above, a maelstrom of twenty-thousand black wings.


“And what, we just leave it to them? My brother’s straw men!” Father pointed to Mother’s scarecrow. “My brother, he doomed us all by bringing them here… by turning my folk into them! My own family, my own flesh and blood…” Father began to sob. “Look what he did to my wife! My wife, who I loved more than anything, making me look at her day and night. There’s not a moment I can leave the house without seeing her face, her eyes, crucified upon a wooden post like some halloween decoration.” Father loaded the shotgun, closing the barrels with a click. A militant, aggressive click. “No more. Today they all die, all of them! Today I take this farm for the living!”


John Bo dropped his glass. The brown liquid spilled atop the dead patch, staining the dirt. He lunged towards my father, bringing him to the ground with his muscular arms. They wrestled in the dirt like pigs fighting over slop. Father’s shotgun remained clutched against his chest. He shoved the gun against John Bo, pushing him aside. Once he stood up, Father swung the butt of the gun into John Bo’s cheek, shattering bone. John Bo fell downward, crushing his fallen glass into shards of a sharp knife. The crows above cackled, taunting, commanding. Father’s nose shot up like a missile, looking at the black mass above. I could see him mouthing words, speaking to them in some unknown language. Far stranger than the runes, far stranger than the cipher. Then, he looked back at the fallen John Bo. A grin flicked over my father’s lips. His tongue swirled around his mouth like a predator stalking prey. He lifted the barrels of his gun, and pulled the trigger, turning John Bo’s head to a red mist among the dirt.


I fell to the ground, covering my eyes. The crows laughed at my misery, laughed at the death of John Bo. “Caw caw!” Yelled Father. The farm spun around me like a floating island in a tornado. Whimpering, I prayed that everything had been a nightmare. That my headless brother was not bleeding into the dirt beside Mother’s scarecrow. Father ran into the cornfields - I heard the barrels of the rifle fall open, two fresh buckshots inserted. He approached my aunt’s scarecrow, aiming the barrels at her head before pulling the trigger. Her pumpkin head, rather than disintegrating into seeds and gourd, dispensed the same red mist, the same gore as John Bo’s living skull. He reloaded the shotgun - and unloaded it into the body. Intestines blew outward in strips of red, blue, and purple. He walked to each scarecrow like a deranged executioner, shooting it, sending guts and gore across the withering corn. My cousin, executed. My sister in law, executed. All of them reduced to shreds of hay, gourd, and blood. I raised my head, screaming for him to stop, screaming for the gun blasts to stop. Father stood at the far end of the corn field, before Chester’s scarecrow. Before he shot it, an intense guise of hatred plagued his face. He shot Chester’s head, erasing the lineage of our farm, deleting our history. The crows swirled madly. They cackled and cawed like thunder rising from the underworld.


Father walked toward me like a rabid dog. Blood dripped from the hair of his chest. It was smeared across his lips like circus makeup, a blood-crazed clown. Father smiled amusingly, reloading the shotgun. He was going to end me then. I was next. That crazed man was not my father. He was a conduit of evil, a puppet of the crows. Between me and him stood one more scarecrow, the last bastion of defense between life and eternal oblivion. Mother’s scarecrow. The two of them faced each other like ancient adversaries. And before I could rise, before I could throw myself in front of her, Father lifted the shotgun to her face and reduced her to red mist. 


A frail string snapped in my mind. The crows laughed above, cheering Father on as their champion. He laughed madly, cackling. My legs wobbled like splintered wooden posts. Gritting my teeth, falling to madness and degradation, I crawled toward the headless corpse of John Bo. Piercing his fallen back were large shards of glass, the glass he had crushed. I pulled the largest, sharpest shard, and hid it behind my back.


Father cawed hysterically, reveling in his victory, towering over Mother’s body. Growling, my face red, I approached him. 


“It was you… you killed Uncle N.” 


That was the last thing I ever said to my father. Upon hearing the accusation, he looked at me one final time with an apocalyptic realization. And before he could raise his gun, I jabbed the shard of glass into the side of his throat. Blood sprayed from his jugular, and he quickly dropped the gun, attempting to cover the wound. He slowly held his soaked hand in front of his face. As if the sight of the blood induced his death; as if the realization of everything that had come to pass had killed him, he collapsed to the ground and died. Together it was his corpse, John Bo’s, and Mother’s forming a triangle. In the middle was myself, panting, hysterical. And above were the crows.


I kneeled before Mother’s scarecrow. Her old clothes were torn apart with bullets. I hugged her, I hugged the scarecrow, crying and shaking. Like ominous thunderclouds, a swirling hurricane above, the crows amassed. From each direction, east, west, north, south, they approached in armies. Apocalyptic riders from hell, large and red eyed. The horde blotted out the sky, and Wither Creek became darker than night. The horde lowered itself, hovering closer and closer to the ground. I rubbed my hand against my mother’s scarecrow, feeling the old fabric of her clothes. In her shirt pocket, something crumpled, just as something crumpled in the cave of Gerta’s Haunt. From her pocket, I pulled a small, folded piece of paper. As I unfolded it, I saw familiar runes. The same ones which had spelled her name, which had spelled all of their names. With the paper in hand, I walked up the steps of the farmhouse. Before I went inside for the last time, I turned to the fields of corn. One by one, crows swooped down to ravage the land. They were like venomous sky serpents, three tongues protruding from their razor sharp beaks. The maelstrom lowered itself, lower and lower, like the black sky was sinking to crush the earth.


Still within my pocket, stained by blood, was my cipher. Already familiar with many of the runes, I transcribed Mother’s letter with my eyes, filling in the gaps between symbols and sentences. 


Dear Chester,


Ever since my death and transformation, I fear for my husband. I left him with a beautiful daughter, and he has that strong young boy, who he sees as a son. But beside all else, he has N. We have N. I remember when this land was nothing but grime and compost. No one would ever have planted a seed in these accursed grounds. But my husband was a talented farmer. From dust he grew a bounty. He turned grey to green. There was only one evil looming about the land. A force that sought to abolish mankind, and starve it to bare bones.


The crows.


Their manipulation is pernicious. I felt it myself, sleeping in that farmhouse. The nightmares as they flew overhead, beckoning us to join them. It was that man, N, who saved us. We always told the children he was their uncle, but none of us truly know who he was. I think he has always lived in the creek, watching over the land, waiting for us. You knew him far before we were born. He flowed with time itself, him and his magics which turned the dead to effigies of repulsion. It was his creations that protected Wither Creek. Without him, we would have fallen long ago.


Fallen to the crows.


But ever since my death and transformation, I have watched my husband succumb to their grasp. He has been consumed by rage. By hatred and anxiety. Tell me Chester, how long before it all goes back to the way it was? To dust and decay; to death and destruction? Is it true, is it as the Necrolyte said, that the world is a neverending cycle of rebirth and death? I must not think too hard on it… as long as we are here, the children are safe. Without us, without N to safeguard the land, it would all fall to them.


To the crows, the crows, the crows…
