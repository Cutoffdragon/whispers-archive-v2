Nightmares had sent shivers up my spine, tiny legs scrambling along the nerves. First there was one cockroach, small and slender, crawling up the side of the shower. I was even able to kill it myself, at 5AM, the helpless beast. Little by little the sightings increased. Roaches climbing up walls, coming out of food boxes. Before long it became a once a day occurrence. Every day, I would wonder when I would see the roach of the day. The thought itself was torturous. Every corner was a black abyss containing unknowable horrors. Every closed door was a hive. I could not obtain peace of mind until the roach of the day was slain. And even after then, the sightings grew to many a day. Before long, Doug reckoned our quaint farmhouse was infested, which he found an odd occurrence for the barrens of Jupiter Crag. We moved from the big city to get away from the roach infested apartments. Here we thought to see coyotes, moles, the occasional condor. My nightmares had manifested into reality, and the roaches grew bigger.


Their bodies became thick, and my mind could discern the meat about them. Stepping on them would offer a crunch, a squeeze, the sound of thickened internals turning to ooze. Their bodies would spew a black bile as they were crushed. On occasion one would possess an oval shaped tail, like a pill hanging from its behind. Doug said these were the gravid ones, the bugs that needed to die the most. Mostly we found the egg sacks empty, deposited by a source of sustenance, ruptured and shriveled to give way to the next generation of the brood.


The swarm was insuperable. I could no longer sleep in my own home. When I laid in bed, all I felt were the legs pattering over my skin, tickling the hairs, entering my orifices. I would throw the covers off of me multiple times per night, turn on the lamp, and expect to see a meaty bugger crawling up my breasts, but there were none. Doug grew annoyed with these frequencies, as it was impossible for him to not wake up as well since he sleeps with me. His ignorance of the menace was admirable, and I wished I could have his brain within my head.


It came to my understanding that the paranoia and the delusions were worse than the infestation. The problem was real and terrifying, but perceived reality became hell. Flecks of black invaders crawling along the walls out of the corner of my eye. Turning on the lights in a dark room, only to see a massive one disappear immediately behind a furnishing. Thinking, and knowing, that the very food you eat, the glass you drink from, has been crawled and oozed along by an unwelcome guest. The bugs drove me to madness, to suicidal ideation.


Doug had a friend from work swing by, Randall. Once he worked for pest control, and while that was an occupation of the past, he was still familiar with the workings. Randall came by, a freelance exterminator, and bombed the house. Our once lovely home was uninhabitable for the day, as the fumes took control of the air. Me and Doug spent the night at a three star hotel by the business town. While the stay was expensive, and quite above our means, even for just one night, it was the greatest peace I had found in months. Even though, when I woke up in the middle of the night, I thought I saw the shadow of one, walking across the wall. The shadow was massive, the size of a small dog. Creeping, crawling slowly across the wall, inexorable in its approach… I awoke sweating, nearly screaming, realizing that another nightmare had taken hold. The sheets had seemed clean the night before, but on that morning there were small, red specks dotting the linen. Blood, blood of our own. I didn’t want to think of the possibilities, my mind was laden as it was. But I didn’t notice the bites on my legs until our ride home, when we returned to the farmhouse.


Corpses of roaches littered our house for days to come. Each passing hour more showed up, fat and wretched, bunched up in death curls. I will never forget when Doug pulled the stove from the wall, the amount of death that laid underneath it. That such an average space could have been occupied by so many roaches was truly appalling. The entire floor underneath, black not with grime or soot, but dead insects and their feces. I wanted to vomit at the sight of it. He removed the fridge, the couch, and other large furnishings by himself, and I hid under the covers. Hoping they had all died, that they were all deceased. A noisome chemical odor lingered about our house, turning my mind paranoid. Doug assuaged me, noting this was the lingering chemicals from the fumigation, and that it was nothing to worry about. But a day later, as this smell abated, an even worse one took its place. 


Decay is the only word that can describe it. Corpses rotting and oozing, festering in the humidity of the coming Summer. The thousands of bodies which still remained unknown to us were infiltrating our lungs with diseased bacteria. Once Doug assured me he had cleaned as many as he could, I walked about the house a bit easier. Cautious, but freely - until I lifted the toilet seat to urinate, and saw the biggest one yet. It was dead, fortunately, but lingered as sort of an omen. As if the swarm was sending me a message, a body left swinging from a tree. The roach floating in the toilet water was the size of a drinking glass. The antennae were two feet long, and the beady eyes were coherent, seeming to contain emotion. That emotion was hatred, contempt for the human race, us who had just slaughtered thousands of their kind. My shriek brought Doug rushing through the bathroom door to comfort me.


In the nights passing, even Doug struggled to sleep. Both of us scratched furiously at our limbs, our bodies and our scalps. Upon awakening I noticed similar red dots, a constellation on our sheets, one that spelled dismay. Doug knew what the signs indicated, but withheld the knowledge to protect me. But I was not an ultimately feeble housewife - upon researching the subject, I came to know well what plagued us now. Bedbugs, small, red and orange. The realization pushed me from the house itself - I ran into the barren desert screaming in my night gown, tears running down my face. When Doug found me, kneeling in the red clay, I begged him for help. Begged him to call Randall again, to have him save us from this terror. I suggested we find somewhere else to stay, a friend, a relative, anywhere. A shallow suggestion, as beneath the panic and trembling, I knew we had nowhere else. Our relatives were either dead or estranged. The few folk we called friends were of too light acquaintance to ask such a favor. Our lives had been tied to the farmhouse, and it had infested us, just as they had infested it. 


Doug begged me to come to bed, but I refused to sleep among the critters. That was their bed now. For an entire night, I paced about the living room, unable to settle upon reading or knitting from a frenzy of thought. As the restlessness grew, I experienced hallucinations, delusions that the roaches had returned. Every way I looked, there was a black shadow in the corner of my eye. As soon as I would turn to confront the being, it would disappear behind a cabinet, as if mocking my very existence. It all became too much; at last I turned off the lights, so that I would see nothing. How fancy a desire that was - a massive shadow lingered above me, larger than any insect of modern times. The appendages resembled human arms and legs - the figure above me, as discerned by my peripheral vision, was a human. Crawling along the ceiling, a faceless being staring directly down at me. My screams woke Doug again as I scrambled for the light switch, and he ran downstairs to admonish me. Doug was growing tired of my weakness, and the infestation had come to enfeeble our twenty year marriage.


To my surprise, it would be Doug who would awaken me the next morning, with a scream equally as shrill. I had fallen asleep on the couch out of sheer restlessness; as I awoke, I thought I felt a crawler on my face, disappearing into my hair as I stirred. Frantically I ran to accompany Doug, a man who I had never once heard scream in all our time betrothed. In the kitchen, Doug sat on the floor, cowered into a corner. His hand was gripping his chest as if he were having a heart attack. He was panting, and his eyes were wide.


“M-Monster!” Doug accused the wall of the kitchen. “Jesus, it - it was huge! It was the size of a rat!”


Like skeletal fingers I no longer had control of, I gripped my own hair, pulling to release it from my scalp. “A roach? Are they back? Are they?”


“They’re back-” Doug panted, sweat trickling from his pale face. “Honey, I need the shovel, the shovel or something blunt. That thing’s so huge, there’s no way it’s hiding from me for long.”


Doug looked up at me, courage returning to his face, the resolve of the man I knew and loved. “Honey, the shovel. It’s in the basement.”


The basement. The word made my ears curl, deflecting the very sound. Dark, dank, the perfect location for insects just as the one Doug described. I refused to go downstairs, just as I always had, even before the infestation. Something about that place hovered above my good thoughts, a pit to hell beneath our very house. Lined with rodent traps, strange rusted machinery, cracked fragmented floors. That basement was the part of this house I often forced myself to forget. As Doug seemingly remembered my aversion to the pit below us, he instead stood to fetch the shovel himself. He would only be a minute. A long, dreadful minute, where I feared I may encounter the blight of this kitchen in Doug’s absence. I listened to every footstep as Doug descended the creepy stairs, into the room where light did not breach. Wielding a chair from the table in my defense, my head jerked in each direction, jumping at just the thought of the roach. The shovel’s metal clanked against the floor beneath, and Doug rose back to the surface with a weapon in hand.



“Ok now,” He held the shovel in one hand, and placed his other on the dreadful stove. “I think I saw him go behind here. Honey, help me pull, then we’ll get him.” But I wanted nothing more than to run back under the bed sheets. That stove, which had been a graveyard for these insects, was the last place I wanted to look beneath. Doug appalled my hesitation, my weakness. “Honey c’mon! I need your help! You want this thing dead or not?”


Anger bore residence in his face, a small hint, growing weary and tired of my apprehension. Gulping, shaking, I approached the stove, gently placing one hand on it, and then the other. I looked at Doug, my face as pale as his had been before, biting my lip. 


“Ready?” He asked, his hand tightened around the shovel. I slowly nodded my head. Doug counted to three, in a low, soft whisper, as we hid from a danger in our home. As each second passed, and three approached like a fatal bullet, we ushered our might to move the stove. As soon as it budged, Doug fell back screeching once more. My mind could not comprehend the horrors that emerged from that stove, as we just barely budged it. I was locked in a moment of sheer terror, the realm between dream and reality, as four roaches, the size of groundhogs, skirted from the stove. The meat on their bodies was unreal, lined with ridges and girth, a shining carapace. They were discolored, containing patches of green and yellow I had not seen before. Their features were misshapen and distorted, like escaped science experiments, mutants from the depths of space. As the four foot antennae of one brushed my ankle, reality grabbed my throat, and I leaped to the table like a cat. The beasts were fast, moving with the speed and agility of a smaller roach, which was disproportionate to their size. Doug scurried back on the floor, the shovel clanking hard as it fell to the floor. All the while, I screamed at the top of my lungs, my eyes bulging from their sockets. 


The four of them dispersed in disparate directions. Doug’s terrified gaze followed one into the living room, disappearing neatly under the couch. Finally the man took to action, grabbing the shovel, and running to the coach. He did not ask for my help this time - I was useless, mummified atop the kitchen table. Doug tackled the couch with his shoulder, sliding it across the room. The massive roach scurried across the wooden floor, its pattering audible. Doug quickly drove the point of the shovel into the back of the insect. As he did the creature's wings unfolded - it had been about to take flight. To its demise, the metal penetrated deep, penetrating the pethorax. A squirt of black bile oozed from its behind, dousing Doug’s shirt. He didn’t care for this in his determination; he lifted the shovel again and brought it down on the insect’s head, stabbing it with the point. Doug repeated this action many times, like a mad killer stabbing a victim to death, beating the husk far past the point of death, until it was a black puddle of bile and dismembered parts on our living room floor.


Minutes of panting, and dissociation followed. As Doug came back to reality, he asked me to help him clean up the mess. But I was unresponsive, curled in a fetal position on the kitchen table, my own death curl.


Doug snapped, unable to deal with my weakness any longer. “Get up and help me you useless bitch!”. His reproach brought way to tears, and I sobbed uncontrollably. Doug regretted his words immediately, his burst of anger bringing about its own tears. Absently and in shock, Doug turned around, his eyes distant. Slowly, like a man in another world, he obtained the cleaning materials and set to work on cleaning up the murder. All the while, I spazzed uncontrollably, contorting and praying for death.


I don’t know what Doug did for the next few hours, though I imagine he sat in a corner, pondering our next course of action. When he approached me, I had calmed down myself, though the prospect of staying in the house any longer was a nightmare of its own. I even contemplated homelessness, a night on the streets. Was that any worse than residing within the same walls as those woodchuck sized roaches? Four of them had come out, only one had been killed. Likely those were not the only four in the house as well.


“I think,” Doug began slowly. “I think they mutated or something… they adapted to the chemicals, they grew stronger.” Doug looked to the ceiling as I listened to his words through muffled glass. “God help us, God help us.”


Despite my prayers for salvation, for liberation from this rotting prison, Doug insisted that he was not ready to abandon the farmhouse just yet. His stance was existential, philosophical even. It tied back to his time in the military, his time during the war. He was a man of jagged edges, obstinate and stubborn, yet very brave. The opposite of me; he contained everything I was not. We left the house for the evening, running errands in town. Normally I would never have gone along for these menial tasks, but any excuse to get out of the house was a good one now. At the pharmacy, Doug purchased the strongest benzodiazepines one could get over the counter. One the drive home, he promised me that he would bring the infestation to an end. He would scrounge up the last of our savings, and afford the best exterminator money could buy. Even though this ordeal would drive us broke, he deemed our comfort and safety to be worth all the money in the world. And I could not have agreed more.


The drugs were strong, and for the first night in months, I felt like I could fall asleep comfortably. The idea of insects crawling over me was no longer an unbearable reality, but something that I could deal with. Though like a shadowy puppeteer, the thought of those massive roaches hid behind the curtains of my drug induced brain, ready to crawl back to the main stage once I regained coherence. I blacked out from the drugs, not knowing when or how I fell asleep in our bed, just that I did. Up until the early hours of the morning, when I awoke from my slumber to hear Doug whimpering next to me.


Silently, like an ill hound suffering from starvation, my once strong husband quivered next to me. Like a small quake through the bed, I could feel his body trembling. Fear held solid in my mind like it had never left, a realization that was murky through the drugs, and crystal clear now, as the effects had waned. Worse, a tingling sensation ran throughout my body.


“Mary,” my husband whispered. “My, my body…” Never had I heard Doug so terrified. “My body is on fire… help me…”


Fire. The word flipped a lever in my own sensation, as I realized that the tingling sensation was fire as well. Slowly, my arm stiff as if my every movement was being watched, I reached towards the bedside lamp, and dimly illuminated the room. Doug’s face was clear now - his eyes were red, beset by a licorice discoloration underneath. I looked slowly down to the covers. The burning grew hotter every second, my body melting beneath the blanket. And in one fell swoop, I threw the covers from our bodies, revealing nothing but moving red dots covering our bodies. As if we were covered in bees, the bedbugs, as bright red as blood, covered every inch below our necks. At once, and in unison, me and Doug screamed at the ceiling. As we jerked out of bed, the bugs began to dissipate, leaping from us in each direction, entering the curtains, the draws, and seeking shelter in the rug. Me and Doug ran out of the front door, into the barren night of Jupiter Crag, swiping at our bodies, our legs, our backs. Doug fell to the ground and began to roll in the dirt, scratching heavily at his skin, leaving claw marks upon himself. I had wished for a body of water to plunge into, but there was nothing by dry dirt in Jupiter Crag. I simply fell to the ground and mimicked Doug. Deep beneath my screams, I thought I could feel laughter. Climbing to the surface, with no right to come from my voice.


That fateful incident marked a turning point in our opposition to the forces at play. No longer did Doug maintain his tough attitude, his stance of standing his ground. We were cowards alike now. Surrendering to nature itself, which has placed a curse upon us and our home. That night we laid in the dirt, screaming and scratching until dawn. There were no neighbors to hear us, to witness our madness. Without going back into the house for a change of clothes, we simply drove away that morning. Drove into town, for any place we could sit and have coffee. Our appearances were scarred and ragged. Both in our pajamas, lacking real shoes or socks. We were both covered in red bumps, which were unbearably itchy. An itch that could not be resisted. As we sat at the diner, ordering coffee repeatedly, the surrounding denizens stared at us with contempt. We looked like drug addicts, absolute scourges to society, itching our ankles, our necks, lacking proper sleep.


The following days were filled as such. Me and Doug wandered from business to business, loitering, reeking from a lack of hygiene. We hung around just long enough to seem a drugged out nuisance, but left just before the authorities could arrive to trespass us. Typically we spent our mornings at a diner. In the afternoon, we would occupy some library, or a laundromat. And at night, as businesses closed, and the average citizen grew increasingly weary of folk such as us, we simply sat in Doug’s truck, where we would sleep through the night. It was poverty, and we were homeless people with a home - one we were simply too afraid to inhabit.


Sleeping in the truck was a difficult task. It was a single cab pickup, with limited room to recline the seats, leaving us to either sleep sitting up, or to lay down on top of each other, our feet curled in the cab. I did not sleep most nights. I would sit in the cab, watching raccoons invade garbage cans from whatever parking lot we chose that night. Spying on real amphetamine addicts smoke cigarette buts from the ground. We were undefended, with no firearm or knife to protect us from the denizens of the night. Many times I awoke Doug, afraid that a nearby outlaw would raid our truck, and attempt to do us harm.


Whether it was an illusion from sleep deprivation, or actuality, I began to grow suspicious that we were being followed. It started with a figure in the night, a black shadow like the one I had seen crawling on the ceiling. Rather than mope along like a beggar, it would stop and stare at us, from a mass of brush. Soon this stalker took form, sitting at a nearby table as we occupied a diner one morning. Doug pleaded for me not to stare at the man, to not draw his attention. But I found it impossible; his appearance was deformed and disturbing. He wore glasses that were too large for his face, with eyes beneath them that pulsed with veins, and popped out farther than usual. Even worse, the pupils were black and beady, bearing no color. Thinning hair spread wildly from his scalp like a mad scientist, and his nose was thick and bumpy, like a pickle. 


“Mary, don’t look at him! You’re just going to entice him!”


But I couldn’t stop. Something about this man spelled danger. Generations of evolution, developing anxiety and foresight to protect humans from men such as this one, was brimming in my skull. Even as I looked away, as I tried to obey Doug, the man’s raspy breathing could be heard throughout the diner. Each breath was heavy and laborious, like he needed assistance to breathe.


He would follow us to each place we loitered. The gas stations, the grocery stores and all. I would enter the bathroom with Doug when he would do his business, as the prospect of being left alone for a second was unthinkable. Like a hawk wasp, this stranger would swoop me up, and bring me to a dark pit, like the basement beneath the farmhouse. Doug had become fed up. That militant fervor brewed in him once more, and he plotted to confront the man. As he stalked us one day from the bushes, Doug marched towards him, his fists balled, his teeth gritted. But the man just disappeared into the night like a phantom effigy. Each attempt to confront him was fruitless. Little did we suspect that the man would eventually find us first.


We had been on the road for almost a month. Likely the farmhouse was so infested at this point, that it was better off left for dead. It was a typical morning; we occupied one of our usual diners, where the owner tolerated our company. As usual, the strange man sat at a table far from us. Me and Doug glowered at the man, his breathing raspy, his attire opulent yet dirty, wearing an old brown suit jacket. To our great surprise, he stood from his booth, and approached ours. I could tell Doug was preparing for a fight as the man sat down at our booth, but his presence was relatively passive.


“Good morning, friends,” The man breathed like a broken air conditioner.


Doug wasted no time interrogating the stranger. “Who are you? Why have you been following us?”


“Ah, yes, my apologies for my off-kilter methods. You see, I was simply taking time to ascertain your situation…”


Me and Doug gave each other a glance of confusion. Which situation was he referring to? Our current life of vagrancy, or the infestation at our home?


The strange man lifted a napkin, and wiped some dribble from the corner of his mouth. “I have overheard your plight quite thoroughly, yes indeed I have. It would seem that you two are experiencing quite the indomitable infestation, is that correct?”


“That’s none of your concern. And we don’t appreciate being spied on.” Doug replied with truculent force.


“Ah yes, once again, my apologies. And perhaps you are right, your problems are none of my business. But perhaps I propose a solution, perhaps I know someone who can exterminate the issue from the very core…”


Doug paused within his head for a moment. “...You an exterminator?”


“No, but I represent an exterminator.” The man cleared his throat, pushing phlegm up and throughout, coughing in our direction. “But unlike other exterminators, my man will get the job done. You see, regulated pest control follows the rules. They use limited chemicals, weak substances that cunning insects can adapt to. We can’t play by the vermin’s rules, that is practically forfeiting the game, eh? No, you need someone who isn’t afraid to break the rules, who can bring the war back to the insects. The man you need is Joel Peskey.”


The man picked up a crumpled, coffee stained napkin, and wiggled his fingers, grabbing for something that wasn’t there. As a waitress passed, he hailed her attention, snorting and coughing, asking her for a pen. Revulsed, the waitress frowned at our table - this weird, insect-like man, and two smelly vagabonds. She dropped the pen, ensuring no contact with the man’s hand was made, and quickly made for another table. The man jotted down a phone number on the crumpled napkin, and slid it across the table.


“Look, we don’t have a lot of money-” The man lifted his finger, interrupting Doug’s worries. “I think you will find that Mr. Peskey is quite flexible, yes, flexible indeed…”


“Give Joel a call. He’ll get the job done.” And the man began to get up, but stopped one last time to look back at us, his eyes bulging especially hard now. “And one more thing - don’t share that number with anyone… don’t tell anyone we had this conversation.” Bowing his head just barely, the man made off through the door of the diner. We would never see him again.


That night, as we sat in the truck eating potato chips, me and Doug debated calling the number. Joel Peskey would not be the first exterminator we have called - far from it. During our tenure as homeless people, we have consulted with many exterminators to little avail. Everyone we contacted was either too expensive for our limited savings, or too cheap and shady to properly get the job done. We figured Joel Peskey would fall into the latter category, given the awkward circumstances of our meeting with the man, and his trailing words - ‘Don’t tell anyone we had this conversation’.


But Doug figured it was worth a shot. I, however, held a truly appalling feeling in my gut. “Something’s not right about this.” I forewarned Doug. “That man was just too weird, following us, stalking us. And why did he tell us not to share the number, or tell anyone we had talked to him? Something is wrong about this. What if this is just a scheme for some man to rob us or kill us?”


The argument made sense to Doug, but in his haggard eyes, there was that glint of desperation. Homelessness had taken its toll on both of us, and Doug wanted badly to be back in our bed. He was willing to try anything at this point, anything to restore our lives. “I’m not gonna let anyone hurt us.” Doug assured me, placing his hand on my shoulder. “Look, if we don’t make the call, we’ll never know. Maybe this is the only guy who can help us. Maybe in a week from now, we’ll be back in the old farmhouse, eating pancakes and bacon again.”


“Maybe…” I whispered, sullen as Doug stepped from the truck, and walked to a nearby pay phone. His call was deceptively short, leaving a bewildered fear across Doug’s face as he walked slowly back to the truck. “Well?” I asked dubiously, expecting that the call had not gone well. “He wants us to meet him at our house. Tomorrow.” Doug swallowed. “He already knew the address.”


Again, the unsettling nature of ‘Joel Peskey’ settled in my subconscious. “We are going to have to go back… to the house.” I murmured, both me and Doug staring towards the pay phone as if it were an unwelcome visitor, as if that man had still been stalking us. “Yea.” Doug confirmed. Now he looked at me and took my hand. “But let’s stay out here for one more night. Then we go back tomorrow.”


That was a sleepless night. I tossed and turned in my seat, feeling a chill from the air, the weather cooler than it had been. Returning to the house was a haunting prospect - roaches lining the walls, eating whatever food was left behind, perhaps even gnawing at the furniture and wallpaper. The last roaches we saw were as big as small dogs. Who knows what size they amounted to at this point.


Joel Peskey requested an early meet up at our farmhouse, the break of dawn. As if the man was as eager to get to work, as we were apprehensive to see our beloved home. We did not stop at a diner that morning. We did not drink coffee or eat anything. We drove back to our home in silence, watching the sun rise as we traversed the desolate, abandoned roads of Jupiter Crag. Though we were late arriving, no other vehicle was seen around the premises. Doug figured that Joel himself was running late; and so we sat in the truck, staring at our house like a monstrosity. Even from this distance, sitting in the truck, I thought the bugs would find me. Already my skin tingled, and those phantom legs tickled the hairs on my skin. And yet, I felt that I would have to use the bathroom soon - and for the first time in my life, I contemplated squatting outdoors. Doug opened the car door. “C’mon, let’s take a look inside. Plus, I don’t want you using the bathroom out here when that guy possibly shows up.”


“Are you sure? I can’t go in there, I…”

“No, c’mon, let’s go.” Doug was stern now, tugging at my shoulder. “Coming back here, it reminds me of who I once was. And what a coward I’ve become. I’m a man surrendering to insects… insects! Of all things! No more, whether Peskey shows up or not, we’re gonna find an exterminator and take our home back. But I’m done letting them control me.”


Doug marched towards the farmhouse without looking back at me. I quickly followed behind, unwilling to be left alone, even in the truck. Not when roaches could crawl from beneath the seats. Not when Joel Peskey could arrive at any moment. Doug stomped up the wooden steps, and inserted our key into the door knob. He turned the key, but there was no click, no resistance whatsoever. “Doors unlocked,” Doug insisted. “Did I forget? Or…” Now grim determination entered my husband's eyes. His hand slowly turned the door knob, pulling the door towards him. He gnashed his teeth together, expecting that behind the door would be a massive humanoid roach, ready to lunge at our throats. But what we opened our home to was a man, standing right behind the door, awaiting our arrival. 


I shrieked, leaping back - in the flash of the moment, I truly did picture an upright cockroach awaiting us. Even Doug gasped, stepping backwards, nearly falling down the steps. The man standing in our home had a hunch, a spine that was twisted and contorted. His head, mostly bald, had stray wisps of hair protruding from it, pointed wildly in each direction. Though his most notable feature was his face coverings. Bright green goggles obscured the man’s eyes. His mouth and nose were covered by an industrial respirator, hooked up to an oxygen tank which rested upon that leaning back of his.


“Doug. And Mary, I presume.” The man greeted us through a malefic voice, distorted by his respirator. Strangely, I did not expect that Doug had given out my name on the phone. Perhaps he had not.


“Joel?!” Doug shifted back to reality. “What are you doing in our house? How did you get in there? Do you think this is really appropriate?”


Joel’s breaths were deep, altered by that same deep wavelength that distorted his voice. “My apologies…” Each sentence was separated by raspy breathing. “I had arrived earlier than expected… I did not want to waste time, you see. Furthermore, your door was unlocked…”


“Bullsh-” Doug stopped for a second, thinking. “Mr. Peskey, I still think this is highly in-”


“Mr. Doug, I have reacted with haste to a matter you consider of utmost urgency. You have abandoned the home for a time, yes? Come within the premises, look at how they have made it their home.”


Peskey receded within the house, allowing a view of the interior. What I saw was not our home anymore. The shell of our home remained, but it looked abandoned for decades, reclaimed by flora, fauna, and poverty. Me and Doug slowly walked inside, allowing a foetid odor to pervade our nostrils. Damp, green moss were scattered throughout the surfaces. A black, speckled substance covered almost every square inch not taken over by moss.


“Is that.. Mold?” I asked demurely.


“Far worse. It is defecation.” Peskey replied without looking at me. Realizing now, exactly what that foul smell was, I almost gagged. The scene was so horrifying, I had either forgotten I had to urinate, or had already done so in my drawers. 


“Mother of God…” Doug walked slowly, each footstep an uneasy testament to his fear.


“There were a great many specimens in the main room when I arrived. I have taken the liberty of disposing of them already. Come now, into the kitchen.”


As I had feared, many of the wooden furnishings, including our kitchen table, had been gnawed away at, a snack for the vermin. Our kitchen resembled the living room in every way, moss, fecal matter abroad. But on the center of the floor, laid across a tarp like dead bodies at a crime scene, were roaches of different sizes. One of them was the size of a full grown dog. I began to cry, shielding my eyes, and lowering to my knees in a detrimentally infected corner. Doug sputtered noises, unable to form words.


“You did well to call me in.” Peskey began. “These are not your average cockroaches, no, far from it. Not of any well documented species. I have been researching this brood for a long time, and finally I have a rare opportunity to combat them. I call them black hole roaches, for they consume everything in their path, are resistant to all forms of pesticide, and grow in mass exponentially.”


Doug’s mouth was agape. “How big can they get…”


Peskey lifted a hand to halt his question. “Do not ask questions in which you can not accept the answer.” Peskey walked along the side of each roach, inspecting them with scientific curiosity. “Regulated pest control is unable to combat this threat. The substances I use are dangerous, and have thus alienated me from my business. My license was stripped long ago, but I have never lost my passion for etymology… You were absolutely right to come to me.”


“Look, Mr. Peskey,” Doug’s voice was quivering. I thought I heard a gag from him as well. “I want to do whatever it takes to get rid of them. I want my damn house back. But you have to understand, I don’t have a lot of money. But I will, one day, I can pay you! I promise, please!”


Peskey ushered a low sigh through his respirator. His eyes, hidden by the alien green goggles, made it impossible to discern any emotion in the man. “What I do, I do for science - not for money. What you have given me here, is an opportunity I have craved for years. To study these creatures in their natural ways. That, Mr. Doug, is payment enough for me.”


Forgetting the hive surrounding him, Doug was overcome with gratitude. “Mr. Peskey, I can’t thank you enough. If there’s anything you need from me, please ask. I still want to pay you what I can as well.”


“I told you Mr. Doug,” Peskey folded his hands behind his back. “Money is not necessary. As for the former request, there is indeed something that I need. One week with this home. Before I exterminate the threat, I would like one week to study the creatures, to document their behavior, mating rituals, reproduction, all of it. Then I will end them - and your home will be as it was.”


Doug sniffled. “Just one week?”


“One week, Mr. Doug.”


And so the pact was formed between us and Joel Peskey. We were to bear poverty for one more week, which given how long we had endured, was not a long time. That following week was lighthearted, even filled with small moments of joy. Excitement hung in the air, as me and Doug were looking forward to resuming our normal lives. Every morning in the diner was filled with laughter and compliments. We walked in the woods, exercised at public gyms, and engaged in activities we were far too depressed to do before. As time does when one is enjoying themselves, the week went rather quickly. Our final day of homelessness was even a bit melancholic - we both expressed a sentiment about how we may miss sleeping with each other in the truck. But we immediately chuckled afterwards, as we realized how shallow the admission was. Neither of us would miss this rueful time of our lives. We could always return to homelessness. But as the infestation had proven, our home was something that could be ripped from us at any time. We were grateful to be able to return.


As the sun rose on a dry morning in Jupiter Crag, Doug pulled the truck into our driveway. From the truck, outside the house, I could already tell that something had changed. The air felt clean as it entered my lungs. The atmosphere held the familiar comfort of home, rather than the malice of an invasive lair. Breathing easily, a smile falling upon my face, I stepped from the truck, Doug taking my hand in his as we walked up the steps, as if for the first time all over again. Doug fumbled in his pocket for the key, but stopped as he did so, and tried the doorknob. As he thought, it was unlocked. In the living room, facing away from us, stood Joel Peskey in a white lab coat. Undulating slightly with each raspy breath, Peskey slowly turned to face us - but neither of us focused on him. We looked around at our house, the living room, clean as a whistle. There was a shine to the wood polish, an abode far cleaner than it had been even before the infestation.


“Joel, you didn’t have to do all of this!” I greeted him with elation.


“What a great job, did you hire cleaners?” Doug asked earnestly.


Joel sighed through the respirator again. There was an air of brooding and depression about the masked man, as if he was sad his week had come to an end. “I did a minor sweep… the moss and the fecal matter disintegrated with the application of my… chemicals. Any bioproduct of the infestation went with it as well.”


Without another word, without holding his hand out for payment, or even expecting a thank you, Peskey began to walk through the front door, his head hung downward.


“Joel,” Doug stopped him. “How’d ya do it?”


Peskey stopped walking, his head still low, looking at the floor. “You can never know, Mr. Doug. They would throw me in prison forever.” And Joel walked down the steps, and around the side of the house. He was gone, seemingly disappearing as he turned the corner of the house. Curiously, I walked onto the porch, looking into the desert for any sign of him. There was no pest control van pulling into the road, or any vehicle of any sort. Just a bale of tumbleweed rolling across the yard, Joel Peskey gone with that same gust of wind.


My steps were still cautious as I walked to the kitchen. My mind could not wrap itself around the cleanliness, the air was intensified with the smell of cleaning materials, disinfectant and soap. It was almost noxious, like a bomb of air freshener had exploded in the house. Convinced that this was a dream, that I was still in bed on that night where me and Doug had been swarmed, I grew uneasy. I feared that I would never be able to live in this house as I once had. Opening the fridge, I jumped backwards at the sight of a brown object, thinking it was moving, skittering along on six legs - but it was just the carton of eggs. I prepared breakfast, anxious with every drawer I opened, inspecting each utensil as I obtained it. No insects were to be seen. Once the eggs were done, and the plates were set out, I pulled my chair out from under the table, looking at the seat, looking underneath it. Nothing. Me and Doug sat and ate breakfast, talking about the news and the weather as we had used to.


Later in the day, I took a shower, while Doug sat comfortably on the very couch that roaches had disappeared under previously. I kept expecting them to crawl from the drain, from the very shower head itself. But I cleaned myself in peace, I became as clean as the shower itself. Flipping my towel in each direction before drying myself, no bugs fell from it. I smiled as I wrapped it around my head. Risking terror, I obtained my toothbrush without apprehension. And to no surprise, it was also clean.


Laying back in our bed was a feeling simultaneously comforting and anxiety inducing. It had been the last place we laid before retreating from our home, on that horrifying night when I pulled the covers up, to reveal…


I pinched my nose, blocking out these thoughts. They were in the past, a bad nightmare to never occur again. At least that was my most dire hope. What a great job Joel Peskey had done. While his methods were a secret, and had garnered him an ill reputation amongst his peers, they were certainly effective. I just couldn’t wrap my mind around what those methods could have been, other than dangerously potent chemicals. What else could they be? An air of mystery hung about the idea of Joel Peskey. I remembered how he had seemingly disappeared around the corner of our house. There was no indicator of any mode of travel. Even the day that we had first met him here, there was no van, no bicycle, nothing. We were just caught by surprise when we opened the door to find him here. There was something insect-like about Peskey, just like there was about that stalker who introduced us to him. Like a roach itself, Peskey just appeared out of nowhere, and disappeared into nothingness behind any surface he could find. A disturbing thought entered my mind - what if Joel Peskey was still here? I was absolutely sure I did not see him leave. What if we had traded one infestation for another, one far worse? Could Peskey be infesting this very house now?


I expressed my concerns to Doug, who was over the entire ordeal already, relaxing in bed like the sheets had never been infested at all. “Peskey’s a smart guy, smart people tend to be weird.” Doug reassured me. “And he probably parked his van behind a boulder or something. He didn’t want to risk it being spotted in the open. After all, the man seemed quite paranoid about being found by authorities.”


Doug’s explanation was comforting, and did seem to make sense, but only for a moment. What boulder would Peskey have parked his van behind? There were no boulders big enough to conceal such a thing for almost a mile. Peskey must have gone to great lengths to conceal his workings here. Yet as we turned off the lamp, and entered darkness, silence, I still felt afraid. No longer did I fear that cockroaches would fondle my legs. I thought now that Peskey would reach his arm from underneath the mattress, grab me by the ankles, and drag me into the basement. These occult thoughts tormented me throughout the night, while Doug was fast asleep. After I found the prospect of sleep futile, I went to the bathroom. Opening the medicine cabinet, I braced myself to see a swarm, pinching my nose again as there was nothing of the sort. I medicated myself with some of the leftover benzodiazepines Doug had purchased, and less than ten minutes later, I felt drowsy, as if anesthetized. But in my dreams, I did not escape the idea of Peskey, his respirator covered face, those goggles which covered his eyes. In the dream, Peskey removed these features, to reveal ones far more disturbing - a proboscis where his lips should have been, dripping with green venom. Red compound eyes behind the goggles, like a heat seeking grid. From his back, where the oxygen tank had rested, protruded a slimy pair of diaphanous wings, unfolding, allowing him to take flight.


My eyes erupted open, shattering the veil of sleep, the drugs dissipating. In the darkness of the room, on the ceiling right above me, was the shadow of a man, Joel Peskey. Just as I had seen it months ago, his arms and legs extended outward, clutching to the ceiling. And as I twitched, showing myself to be awake, he skittered across the ceiling, pattering against the wood as his hands scurried in quick, unnatural movements. Peskey disappeared into the closet, crawling through the crack of the door, flattening himself to squeeze through the opening. I screamed, frantically shaking Doug.


“What?! What, are they back? Are the roaches back?” Doug threw the blanket off of us, turned the lamp on, and scanned the sheets, finding no insects. Confused, he looked at me as I shivered, curling into a ball. “Mary? What is it, what did you see?”


“He was on the ceiling!” I yelled. “He was crawling like a bug! He’s still here, don’t you see?”


“Who?” Doug questioned.


“Peskey, Joel Peskey!”


Doug sighed, a long and loud exhalation. “Mary, Joel Peskey was not on the ceiling…”

“Yes, I saw him, he went into the closet! He’s in there now!”


Doug lifted himself from the bed, casual and without concern. “If I check in the closet, will you stop? Will you admit it was all a nightmare and let me sleep?”


Before I could answer, he was already walking towards the closet door. I wanted to beckon him to grab a weapon, to get the shovel from the basement first, but he swung the door open before I could do so, revealing the mostly empty closet. Our bare belongings, jackets, shirts, and pants hung as they would normally. There was no sign of Joel Peskey. Cautiously, I crawled across the bed, looking closely into the closet. Doug had an insuperable look about him, disappointment in my irrational fears. 


“There!” I said, pointing to an open crease along the bottom of the wall. “He must have disappeared through there.”


“Mary,” Doug began with exasperation. “How would a human fit through there? Not even a cat could crawl through that.”


“Because…” I whimpered. “I saw him squeeze through the top of the closet door, without even opening it… He’s like a roach himself…”


Doug stomped to the bathroom, returning with the bottle of medication I had just taken earlier. I swatted at the bottle, telling him that I had already helped myself, to which he plopped himself back on the bed restlessly. “Mary, I understand you’re afraid. After what we had to go through, there’s going to be some lingering anxiety. I think we should call someone tomorrow, a therapist. After a traumatic event such as the infestation, a lot of people could use someone to talk to.”


I didn’t protest - how could I after everything I said? I knew how crazy I sounded, but I had also been well aware of what I had seen. Either Doug was right - I was truly losing my mind. Or Joel Peskey had been on the ceiling, looking down at me as I slept, looking to make a meal of me. The remainder of the night was hardly filled with sleep. As I watched the sun rise, I couldn’t bear to lay in bed any longer. Doug snored next to me, deep in sleep that I could not obtain. The bathroom called to me, as it often does in the morning. Creeping down the hall, I came to the bathroom, noticing that the door was closed. Me and Doug never closed the bathroom door, not unless one of us were using it. And I had just seen Doug, fast asleep in bed. I placed my hand on the doorknob, turning it, and pushing the door slightly. Suddenly, something pushed back, from the other side. Someone was in the bathroom, and did not want to be found. From underneath the door, a large shadow moved, accompanied by a low clicking noise, like insects in congregation. Backing into a wall with a thump, I knocked a picture frame down, shattering the glass as it hit the floor. By the time Doug arrived to comfort me, I was bawling, tears dampening my pajamas.


“It’s never going to end…” I wailed. “I just want to die already!”


“Shhh…” Doug put his arm around me. “We’re going to get you help Mary. Everything is going to be ok.”


Doug accompanied me throughout the house for the entirety of the day. Already, I was back in the same situation as before. Seeing things that were not there, creeping crawlers in the corner of my eye at every turn. Except this time they were not roaches - they were fetid humans containing insect features. I sat across from Doug as he phoned a psychiatrist, setting a time for our first appointment.


“Tomorrow at 10 AM?” Doug asked. “Perfect. Yes, yes, I have insurance. Yes, it should cover it. Thank you.” He hung up the phone, looking gently into my eyes. “Everything’s going to be alright Mary. Everything’s going to get better.”


The phone sat in front of him. There was a call I was thinking of making as well - one to the police, to find out more about Joel Peskey. Something was not right here, and I knew it. Doug could accuse me of being crazy all he wanted, but I knew what I had seen. Joel Peskey was on the ceiling last night, and this morning he was in the bathroom. He was still here, he had been here all along, from the very moment Doug gave him our address. We had fallen into a trap. Peskey was secretive for a reason, because he was illegitimate, a criminal. Likely he used his pest control front as a way to get himself through the door, concealing his lies with his stories about dangerous research methods. Yet, nothing explained how he had actually gotten rid of the insects. I needed more information. I needed a moment alone from Doug.
Eventually that moment would come, as every day Doug would inevitably sit in the bathroom for quite some time, to do his business. “You going to be okay out here for five minutes?” Doug asked me, patronizingly. I shook my head in response, and Doug walked into the bathroom, the very room that I had known Peskey was in. Without wasting a moment, I tip-toed downstairs and picked up the phone. A magnet on our refrigerator held the non-emergency number for the police, which I dialed in haste. After entering the proper extensions, a grim voice greeted me on the phone.


“I believe there is a wanted man in my home.” I said, speaking as quietly as possible. “Are you looking for a man named Joel Peskey?”


There was silence, unsettling phone static taking over the line. Finally, the man responded.


“You’ve seen Joel Peskey?”


“Yes.” I whispered. “I hired him for pest control in my home, and I think he’s still here… he never left.”


The man’s breathing intensified. “Ma’am, this infestation you had recently, were the insects rather large?”


My voice began to quiver. “Yes… yes they were. Please, help me…”


Another interlude of phone static ensued. I could no longer keep my tears at bay, crying softly through the line. “Hello?” I asked after a moment. A clink followed, as the man hung up the phone. Holding the phone like a foreign object, I sat in disbelief. An officer of the law had just hung up on me, or perhaps, have the phone lines been cut? I went to dial the non-emergency number once more, but as I thought frantically, I opted to dial the emergency number. Before I could press the last button, Doug reached the bottom step, walking into the living room. He stared at me as I held the phone in my hand.


“Who are you calling?” He asked seriously, his face as hard as a hammer.


I had been caught red handed. I wanted to lie, to tell him I was calling family, or a friend, but Doug had trained himself to see through my lies. Any effort to conceal my intentions was futile, the truth was bound to come free. “The police.” I said, slowly lowering the phone back onto the hook. “Because Joel Peskey is in this house, and you just haven’t seen him, and I’m sorry you don’t believe me. But Doug, we are in danger…”
Absent of any retort, Doug went upstairs. I placed my palm into my hands, pondering Doug’s resentment, and the phone call to the police. Likely, the police were already on their way. Even though the call had been dropped, they could trace my address and respond to the emergency. The operator’s voice when I mentioned Peskey was just aghast, bound with fear. I remember how it made my spine shiver, a lingering sentiment which chilled me at the very table. The sun set upon Jupiter Crag, falling beneath the red clay horizon. Nights had become increasingly ominous in my home. I never looked forward to their silence anymore. Once there had been peace and tranquility in the darkness, but now there only passed shadows and hallucinations. Many infesting insects became more active at night - I would never forget how the moon signified the start of their dominion.


Above my head, raucous bangings occurred. Doug was making a fit, seemingly tossing objects, slamming drawers, and dropping things. From below, I thought I could hear his mumbling, the low cursing under his breath. But I hear a lot of things that weren’t real. Making my way to the staircase, grabbing the railing and looking up into the dark upper hall, I faintly called Doug’s name. No response was given, but I was sure of the morose mumblings now. A slam rang through the house, as something heavy hit the floor. I walked up the steps on my tip toes, as if approaching a monster. Once at the top, I called Doug’s name again. No answer. From our bedroom a single light emitted into the hall. Peering into the room, hanging around the corner, I witnessed Doug packing a suitcase. At first I feared he was leaving me - the paranoia and delusions had driven him from me, as I always surmised they once would. But the clothes he was stuffing into the luggage, cramming without order, were not his - they were mine.


“Doug?” I said audibly, garnering no answer once again. “Doug!”


Finally, he looked up, his face florid, bearing fangs. He breathed heavily, holding one of my skirts in his hand as if he had been caught at a crime scene.


“What are you doing?” I asked meekly.


Through grating breaths, Doug responded: “Mary, this has gone too far. We can’t wait for therapy to help, you need institutionalization.”


“Institutionalization?!” I yelled, the very word a curse.


“Yes, you’re going, and you don’t have an option. Seeing people that aren’t there, calling the police about it, what’s next Mary? How long before you do something dangerous?”


Doug looked back at the dresser, thinking of packing more, but decidedly zipping up the luggage. Hauling it over his shoulder, he approached me in the doorway, the two of us in a standoff.


“Mary, step aside.” He said sternly.


“Doug, no, please, this isn’t right. I’m not crazy, I’m not-!”


“Mary, stop!” he interrupted loudly. “Look, I love you, but I’m scared too. I’m scared of you! Not of the bugs, not of some strange exterminator, I’m afraid that you’re going to go over the cliff, do something drastic to either me or yourself! Now step aside, do it!” and without asking, Doug moved past me, his shoulder shoving me, nearly knocking me over.


He rushed down the stairs with the luggage, and I pursued him in a fit of tears. I begged him to stop, to slow down, to give me another chance. I promised I wouldn’t see anything else, that I wouldn’t wake him up in the night any more, but his mind was set. Never had he bore such a look of grim determination. I threw my arms around him, attempting to halt him with my weight as he reached the dark kitchen. It was no use - Doug trucked along, dragging me like useless baggage. Feeling about the kitchen wall in the dark, Doug groped for his keys upon the ring, unable to find them. He flipped on the kitchen lights, and I could feel the shockwave through his body, the thump as the luggage fell to the floor in awe.


Four revolting figures stood upright in our kitchen. Three of them were bright red cockroaches, humanoid, wielding multiple arms, standing on their legs, looking at us with a human expression. In the middle of them stood someone not quite man, but not quite insect - undoubtedly Joel Peskey. He looked exactly as he had in the dreams, with added revulsion. His mask was gone to give way to the dripping proboscis, the beaming compound eyes. And indeed, a wet pair of wings hung from his back. Hairs jutted out from his arms - all six of them. The scream I sought so desperately to set loose could not find its way. I was a mute banshee, my mouth open to usher a wail, with only a dead wind blowing forth.


Doug fell onto the floor, nearly fainting. Peskey looked up at us, the proboscis twitching with intrigue. “Ah, if it isn’t our gracious hosts.” He said with a voice that was even more malefic without the respirator. His head and arms twitched, cocking in multiple directions each second, mimicking the upright roaches who stood as his guard. “I must thank you for calling me here, korek kwehah amuckl-lik-lik, Without your call, the plan would never have gone into action, mokokoroso-ke-lik-lik-lik.” 


“What- wh-” Doug’s fingers dug into the wood beneath him, clawing splinters, filing his nails. He looked up at me with a damnable expression of remorse, of acknowledgment of my fears. One of the cockroaches, the antennae swiftly brushing the countertops, clicked and buzzed in an alien language, communicating with the brood.


“Byurtzz-lik ka-ko-roshty. A’zzz’ylto-lik-ca-ke-ke-ke.”


“Ah, yes,” Peskey said in acknowledgment. “They have fulfilled their purpose - Zy’rot ka-lek-ka-ka - but there is one more thing they can indeed do for us, - Cha, -kelosk ma’ta vorose - as the brood, yes, ka-lek-lik-lik, we are quite hungry…”


Peskey’s hands rubbed together like a fly, plotting, looking at us as a drop of acid fell from his proboscis, burning the wood beneath. The roaches at once lunged towards me and Doug, moving with insectoid speed, dropping to their legs and skittering towards us in a flash. Screaming, I flung myself backwards towards the couch. Doug was not so fortunate. As he was on the floor, he had little time to get up before the insects could seize him. Doug wailed, praying for mercy as the bugs lifted him, scurrying towards the basement door. At first they tried to squeeze Doug through the bottom of the closed door, attempting to slide him through as they would themselves. But his head repeatedly bashed against it, unable to penetrate the slim opening. Then the roaches bashed the door downward with force unknown for an insect. The door flew off its hinges, sliding down the decrepit basement stairs, and in the following moment, Doug and his screams disappeared into the darkness below.


“Doug, no!” I reached my hand out for him.


“Kya-kya, do not worry my dear, they will be back for you…” The wet, chittery voice emerged from behind me. Without turning around to face Peskey, I got up and sprinted for the kitchen, diving for the cabinet beneath the sink. I heard the chitters and clicking right behind me, and immediately felt the hairy feelers as they clutched my ankles, pulling me. Managing to hold on to the cabinet door for dear life as I resisted the pull, I saw the instrument of Peskey’s demise within my reach. A can of gaseous death, which I had sprayed many roaches with in previous months- Raid. The can was in sight, but out of reach, as my body was stretched, resisting the inevitable pull of Peskey behind me.


“Zha, kelyk-toryn ga’lept tora va,” Pesky clicked. The worst burning sensation I had ever known bit my ankle. Acid from Peskey’s proboscis engorged my calf, liquidating my skin, and the tendons, as Peskey began to slurp the contents through his straw-like mouth. As I could feel my calf disintegrating, entering Peskey’s stomach, I thought of death, death from the sheer pain itself. But adrenaline kept me alive, and the thought that Doug was in danger, facing a fate far worse than myself. Peskey’s hunger presented an opportunity. As he gorged himself, his pull weakened, and I managed to pull myself within reach of the raid. Grabbing the can, I turned towards Peskey, catching a horrifying glimpse of his proboscis inside my acid covered leg for just a moment. Thinking no further, I unleashed the spray upon his face. 


“ZZZYY’ROOOOOOOOOT!” Peskey vibrated, reeling backwards, placing his six arms over his face as it bubbled, tearing at his chitinous skin. 


Leaping upward, I was still able to stand on my injured calf just barely, on account of the adrenaline. The basement door was nonexistent, leaving only a gaping gap down into the depths. Resolving to go downward for the first time in years, I stood hesitantly at the threshold, imagining the monstrosities below. But Doug is down there, and they are eating him, I thought to myself. Throwing away the old Mary, the coward that could not even look a mouse in the eyes, I entered the basement, limping down the steps with the can of weaponry in hand. 


As I reached the bottom, the basement was pitch dark, filled with a clacking ambience. Whispers of that strange language echoed throughout the walls, and a smell from the deepest abysses of the ocean filled the air. I flicked on the small light, revealing the empty contents of the basement. There were no anthropomorphic roaches, and no sign of Doug. A massive hole into the ground sat in the center of the basement, a purple radiance leaking from it like wisps of smoke. The eerie whispering emerged up from the hole, and flickering hairs waved about at its sides like tentacles. The passage looked like the mouth of a giant worm, and I imagined teeth lining the passage way below. It was utterly foetid, a product of the most disgusting creatures on the planet. But I knew that Doug was down there, in the abode of the invaders.


The hole did not go straight down, but declined on a slope, which allowed me to enter without falling. As I thought I was walking into pure darkness, I noticed that patches of dark purple luminescence shone deep in the cave. Just barely, I could make out my surroundings, but the dim light distorted my view, making everything look vile. Feeling along the walls, they felt organic, slimy, like I truly was in the bowels of some great beast. The floor was thick, and consisted of a mud like substance, which my shoes sank into with each step. My mind supposed this was feces, but I did everything to blot out that thought. I clutched the can of Raid with both hands, holding it out in front of me. The clattering grew louder, and the familiar pattering of legs echoed through the hive. The path split off into manifold tunnels, with no indication of which way to go. I only followed the largest of the tunnels, as I supposed this led to the main chamber - the only place I could imagine Doug being taken. Every minute or so, the paths diverged and converged, a maze of slime and ooze.


My neck tingled, as a long thin hair brushed against it. Pattering clambered above me rapidly, and I felt the mass of a body rush just overhead. Quickly I ducked, holding my hands atop my skull.


“Zorot or’ kyek zewil cha lek kik-il lek kil-kil…”


Whatever creature had been above me disappeared down the tunnel. I had no doubt that it had sensed my presence, and that every minute I trespassed in their hive, my life was in danger. Unmoving, whimpering, I began to whisper to myself. “Doug… please, where are you? Doug, I can’t live without you…”


As if in response to my pleas, a shrill cry of terror traveled through the tunnels. A blood-curtling shout, like someone being burned alive, shattered my ears. No insect would have created that noise - it was Doug, and he was in great pain. The cry set me into a frenzy, and I ran down the tunnels, pushing my aching calf without regard. Each intersection I came to, I followed the largest opening, until the screams repeated themselves, dying roars of agony. I began to follow the noise, the voice growing louder as I descended each passage. Pressure clasped my ears - I was deep within the earth now. At many points, I felt other creatures crawl by me, fondling me with their diseased hairs. Ignoring each passing disturbance, I broke into a sprint, my calf wailing for respite. 


Down the path, the purple radiance intensified, as well as the ambience. The main chamber was ahead, and I could feel the atmosphere shift with its gravity. The air became unbearably humid, sticky with bacteria that invaded my lungs. Breathing was difficult, and my lungs heaved as I exhausted my energy. Now the tunnel opened into a massive chamber the size of an auditorium, with something massive at the center. A titanic cockroach, the size of a freight ship lay on its massive stomach. Like a conveyer belt, the smaller roaches spoonfed bits of flesh and bile to the queen. As it gorged on the offerings, the sound of its chewing was indulgent and gluttonous, like savoring honey. My heart pounded, and my feet planted themselves in the mud. I was frozen with fear, and could not move a muscle, as a massive ootheca was spat from the queen’s behind, protruding with a wet farting noise. Empty egg sacs littered every corner of the chamber, and thousands of infant sized roaches waited alongside them. The workers, which were the size of humans, tore apart these empty ootheca, and fed them back to the queen. A minute later, another grotesque fart gave way to an egg sac - only seconds later, it would rupture, with juveniles crawling from it like a swarm of ants. The queen was reproducing at a rapid pace, a new egg sac every minute. The antennae were thick and long, like beanstalks, curling along the ceiling of the chamber. I did not see the face of the queen, and did not want to - I only saw the purple ooze dripping from the mouth and eyes.

I watched in a dreamlike state as the workers pulled pieces from Doug, tearing his eyes, his cheeks, and every muscle, carrying the meat to the queen. Only for another egg sac to be farted forth a moment later, Doug’s legacy incarnate. This was what had become of the man I loved. This was his fate.


“Do not worry, va sek, va isk,” Peskey said, appearing behind me. As I looked at him, his face turgid and burned, I felt I was wearing goggles. The entire world wavered like a psychedelic trip, as reality shattered before me. “Your turn will come my dear.” Peskey assured me.


Peskey walked forward, rubbing his hands together, admiring the queen. “Beautiful, isn’t it?” He asked me. “When I heard of your plight, I knew I had found them - the Black Hole cockroaches. They are not of this world… Bek ja isk… but for centuries, the species had sought a proper home to reproduce. They just needed a home for the queen, and while you and your husband were away, I ensured the species' growth, their survival…”


“But there was one more thing that was needed… Ma’ tont, il kesk,” Peskey turned to me, fluttering his wings. “...A king, to help propagate the brood.”


Joel Peskey, the king. He had been mating with the fattened monstrosity before us. Once, the queen had possibly been as small as a mouse. Fate had taken hold of us, and Joel Peskey had manipulated our lives in his favor.


“What…” I asked through my stupor. “What are you…?”


“We are the Black Hole Cockroaches, the Zzz’ront’tal. We have always existed, since the primordial times, always waiting for our moment. We are far superior to you humans… We adapt and evolve in a matter of days, shaping ourselves to rule the world, reproducing by the millions.” 


“Zyaar, il-kek-ka-kaka-ka….” Joel Peskey clattered at the ceiling. He was laughing. “You humans had always thought you inherited this world, that you were the apex predator. But how it had taken such little time for us to usurp your throne, how feeble your race has proven itself to be. But that is evolution, my dear. That is nature at its finest.”


Peskey observed Doug’s corpse on the ground. The screams had ceased, he was dead now. The roaches still pulled meat from his exoskeleton, even feeding the bones to the queen. “Humans are so picky in what they eat, ka-lik-lik, such an evolutionary flaw. But at the same time, they are so nutritious…”


Peskey waved one of his arms at me. “Yuk okta yukal. Dispose of her.”


Hundreds of roaches darted towards me, dropping to their legs. “No!” I yelled. “Stay back!” With the can of Raid, I sprayed a circle around me, creating a shield of chemicals. The roaches writhed as they came into contact with the border, shrieking with their high pitched tunes. Backing towards the tunnel entrance, I continued to spray in front of me, until I broke into a sprint, running back into the tunnel. The ground quaked as the weight of thousands of patterings legs ensued in pursuit of me. As I ran, I held the can of raid behind me, my finger pressed down on the release, leaving a trail of Raid in my wake. The roaches complained loudly, unable to follow me through the tunnel. In front of me, I could just barely make out their bodies jumping down in front of me, attempting to block me. They fell before a blast of Raid, sprayed directly in their faces. Each roach grasped at their face as the skin melted from it, falling to the ground. The spray seemed to serve as a temporary deterrent. But it was not enough to kill them.


My heart grew light as I felt the can grow lighter. My feet were moving faster than I could balance myself, as I fell into the mud multiple times. Relentlessly, I sprayed all around me, whether a roach approached me or not. It was a frenzy, and I was a pesticide wielding berserker. Continuously, I followed the largest tunnel, gasping, running for dear life. The spray died down to a sputter, with only empty air coming forth, as I reached the mouth of the hive, and emerged back into the basement. After running upstairs, and entering the living room, my soul left my body. Hundreds of roaches, the size of humans, dogs, tigers, stared at me in the living room. They came from the stove, the couch, the very floorboards themselves. Without thinking a moment longer, I charged full steam at the front door, taking it off the hinges with a shoulder tackle, falling down the steps of the front porch. My face landed in the red clay, clogging my nostrils, stinging my eyes. The entire farm house erupted, roaches bursting from the ceiling, the supporting beams collapsing behind me. Glass shattered, and the entire house turned bright red, completely overtaken by the carapaces of insects.


Scrambling upwards, I ran into the desolate desert of Jupiter Crag. Running past the truck, past the street, with no destination. I jerked my head back once, and saw a tidal wave of bright red bodies pursuing me relentlessly. Looking up into the night sky, embracing the moon, I said one last prayer, one last plea for my life. The moon and stars were blurry, the sky possessing a plastic reflection. Ahead of me, that same obscurity formed a perimeter, like glaring through a dome of plastic. And indeed, I was surrounded by a globe. 


I skidded to a stop, almost crashing into the plastic dome surrounding my plot of land. A giant tube connected the interior of the dome to a large, whirring machine. Outside were trucks, barriers, machinery, and thousands of men. Soldiers and scientists of all occupations stood at attention, being drawn to me as I approached.


“Help!” I ran up to the plastic, banging my fists on it. “Help me! Help me please!”


They ran up to me, just inches away, separated by the barrier. Their looks were pitiful and contemptuous, some of them glaring at me like I was a pet in a cage. Then, as they saw the wave of roaches approaching, they began to yell and scream. Furiously I banged against the dome, attempting to shatter it, to set myself free.


The rabble drew a scientist to my location, the man in charge of the operation. “Enough enough!” he said with a heavy accent. Just as Peskey once was, this man was clad in goggles and a respirator.


“Help me! Let me out of here!” I pleaded to the man.


He sighed, placing his palm against mine on the wall of the dome. “Ma’am, I’m afraid we cannot do that.”


“No! Please! Please!” I banged harder.


“Just think of your contribution to humanity.” The scientist said with melancholy in his voice. “As much as I would like to save you, I cannot open the dome. But know that you are a hero. If it were not for your phone call, we would have never been able to contain them before it was too late. I am sorry, ma’am.”


My palm slid downward. All hope dissipated from within me. The looks on their faces said everything - they were not going to let me free. One last time, surrendered to defeat, I turned around. The horde was upon me, and they covered my body, grabbing every inch of me with their disgusting legs. The men behind the dome leapt backwards as the horde pressed itself against the dome. Some of them covered their faces, unable to watch as I was torn apart. Some of them saluted, a single tear falling down their cheek.


The horde pulled me backward, back towards the remains of what once was my lovely farmhouse.
